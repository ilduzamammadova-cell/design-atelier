# Sony Design

**Essence:** Miniaturisation as a value, technology shrunk until it becomes personal, carried, and owned by one person.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Pocketable is the product | Sony's identity was built on making things small: the TR-63 transistor radio (1957) was sold as pocket-sized; the Walkman TPS-L2 (1979) made stereo listening portable; the Discman D-50 (1984) shrank the CD player to near disc size. | **Product:** set the size target before features; the case is the spec. **UI:** a "pocket" constraint, the core task must work in a 360 × 640 viewport, one-handed, in under three taps; desktop is the expansion, not the origin. |
| G2 · The dedicated physical shortcut | The first Walkman had two headphone jacks and an orange "hotline" button that lowered the music so two listeners could talk, one social function given its own control. | **Product:** promote one human, social function to a dedicated button with a signal colour. **UI:** one persistent, colour-coded shortcut for the most human action (talk, share, pause for someone), a floating control in a reserved accent (#F28C28) used nowhere else. |
| G3 · Colour for personal tech | The yellow, water-resistant Sports Walkman line (1980s) and the bright *My First Sony* range for children (late 1980s) used colour to say who a device is for and where it goes. | **Product:** colour as a statement of context (outdoor, child, studio). **UI:** context themes, e.g. an outdoor/high-glare mode with yellow-on-black (#FFD400 on #111) and larger targets, selected by situation, not mere preference. |
| G4 · Black precision and the monolith | Trinitron-era TVs, professional audio and the PlayStation 2 (2000, black monolith that stands upright or lies flat) used black, fine ribbing and precise edges to signal seriousness. | **Product:** a dark, ribbed, orientation-neutral object. **UI:** dark UI with fine parallel line textures (1px ribs at 4px pitch) for professional modes; layouts that work in both portrait and landscape without a "preferred" orientation. |
| G5 · Iconic controller grammar | The PlayStation controller (1994) labels buttons with four abstract shapes (triangle, circle, cross, square), a vocabulary independent of language. | **Product:** language-free symbols for a fixed set of actions. **UI:** a 4-symbol action vocabulary mapped to the four most common commands, consistent across all screens and input methods (touch, keyboard, controller), each with its own colour. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** Walkman blue and silver (#3A5BA0, #C4C7CC) with hotline orange (#F28C28), Sports yellow (#FFD400), Trinitron black (#121212), PlayStation grey (#B5B5B5) and button colours (green, red, blue, pink).
- **Type:** Sony's own wordmark and clean technical sans on hardware. Free equivalents: Barlow, Saira, M PLUS 1p for Japanese text.
- **Form:** rectangles with precise chamfers, ribbed textures, aluminium, compact enclosures, ergonomic grips.
- **Composition:** dense but disciplined control clusters; labels and icons aligned to strict baselines.

## Attitude

Sony's founders, Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita, pursued products no one had yet imagined and believed design was a central part of creating them. Making technology small, personal and emotionally desirable was the strategy, not a styling layer.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `bang-and-olufsen`, `teenage-engineering`, `joe-colombo`.
- **Productive tension with:** `kenya-hara`, Sony's tech density with Hara's emptiness: compact devices whose UI says almost nothing until asked.
- **Clashes with:** `hella-jongerius`, her irregular craft surfaces fight Sony's machined precision.

## Don't copy

The Walkman TPS-L2 form, PlayStation button symbols and controller silhouette, the PS2 monolith, Sony and PlayStation logotypes, AIBO's form. Take pocket-first and the dedicated social shortcut.
