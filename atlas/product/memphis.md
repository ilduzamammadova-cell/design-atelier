# Memphis

**Essence:** A deliberate collision of cheap patterns, primary shapes and clashing colours that declared functionalism over and taste negotiable.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Printed laminate as primary material | Memphis elevated plastic laminate (with Abet Laminati) — printed with squiggles, speckles and grids such as Sottsass' *Bacterio* — from kitchen-counter filler to the star surface. | **Product:** treat a cheap, flat, printable skin as the luxury layer. **UI:** pattern fills as first-class tokens (`--pattern-squiggle`, `--pattern-speckle`) applied to whole surfaces — sidebars, cards, empty states — with text always on a solid plate above the pattern to keep legibility. |
| G2 · Each part a different material and colour | Pieces such as Sottsass' Carlton room divider (1981) and Michele De Lucchi's lamps assemble every component from a distinct colour or finish, so the object reads as a stack of characters. | **Product:** colour-code every sub-component individually. **UI:** compound components where each sub-part takes its own fill (header coral, body mint, footer black-and-white pattern); good for playful onboarding, games, kids' and creative tools — not for dense data. |
| G3 · Primitive geometry, off-axis | Cones, spheres, cylinders and zigzags are assembled at odd angles and scales; legs tilt, shelves cantilever unexpectedly. | **Product:** basic geometric primitives assembled asymmetrically. **UI:** decorative primitives (circle, triangle, squiggle, half-disc) placed on a hidden grid but rotated (±8–15°) and overlapping card edges; one primitive per card corner max. |
| G4 · Clash as a palette rule | Memphis chose combinations taste rejected — pink with black-and-white speckle, mint with red, yellow with lilac. | **Product:** palettes defined by deliberate dissonance. **UI:** a palette of 6–8 saturated, mutually clashing hues (#FF5A8A, #00C2A8, #FFD23F, #2D2AE0, #111111, #F7F3E8) with AA-tested text-on-colour pairings defined per fill; neutral reading surfaces protect long text. |
| G5 · The group as a brand event | Memphis launched as an exhibition (Milan, September 1981), with a name taken from a Bob Dylan song and a roster of international designers; press and spectacle were part of the design. | **Product:** release as a collective, themed drop. **UI:** seasonal or collaborative "drops" — limited-edition themes made by guest designers, each with its own pattern set, released with an event page. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** hot pink (#FF5A8A), mint/aqua (#00C2A8), yellow (#FFD23F), cobalt (#2D2AE0), red (#E63946), black-and-white speckle and squiggle, terrazzo-like grains.
- **Type:** playful geometric display and Memphis-era logo lettering. Free equivalents: Syne, Righteous, Bungee (sparingly), Hanken Grotesk for text.
- **Form:** laminate-clad boxes, cylinders, cones, zigzag supports, terrazzo, neon tubes, ziggurats.
- **Composition:** totemic and asymmetric; deliberate imbalance; every part a separate shape.

## Attitude

Memphis argued that design is a form of communication and culture, not just function — and that "good taste" was a class code worth breaking. It was short-lived by intention, a provocation that opened the door to postmodern pluralism. Founding figures included Ettore Sottsass, Michele De Lucchi, Martine Bedin, Aldo Cibic, Matteo Thun, Nathalie Du Pasquier, George Sowden, Marco Zanini and the critic Barbara Radice.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `ettore-sottsass`, `postmodern-new-wave`, `pop-art`.
- **Productive tension with:** `dieter-rams` — a Rams-calm functional core with one Memphis surface (pattern-skinned hero panel) is a strong, under-used formula.
- **Clashes with:** `ulm-school`, `kenya-hara` — mutual negation; mixing them usually produces mush.

## Don't copy

Sottsass' Carlton bookcase and Bacterio pattern, Du Pasquier and Sowden textile patterns (still licensed), Masanori Umeda's Tawaraya ring bed, Peter Shire's specific chairs. The generic "Memphis revival" (pastel squiggles + confetti) has itself become a cliché — use one gene, not the costume.
