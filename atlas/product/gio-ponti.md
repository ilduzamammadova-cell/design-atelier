# Gio Ponti (1891–1979)

**Essence:** Lightness as a moral and a pleasure — thin, tapered, faceted forms, decoration laid on plain bodies, and a whole culture of design argued in print.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Superleggera: weight as a target | The Superleggera chair (Cassina, 1957) refines a traditional Chiavari chair until it weighs about 1.7 kg, with slim legs of triangular section. Lightness is measured, not implied. | **Product:** set a mass budget and remove material until it is met. **UI:** treat weight as a spec: page-weight and JS budgets per route, 1px structure lines at ~15% contrast, no heavy shadows, thin tapered dividers. Review question: can anything else be lifted out? |
| G2 · The diamond, the finished form | In *Amate l'architettura* (1957) he defined *la forma finita*: a closed, light form that admits no additions or extensions. The elongated hexagon "diamond" recurs in his plans (the Pirelli Tower, Milan, 1956–60, with Pier Luigi Nervi) and facade tiles. | Primary cards and badges use a chamfered (cut-corner) outline instead of rounded corners. Components are "finished forms": a closed set of variants, and new needs get a new component rather than an extra slot bolted onto an old one. |
| G3 · Tiles as a combinable system | For the Parco dei Principi hotel in Sorrento (1960) he drew a set of tiles in white, light blue and dark blue that combine into dozens of floor patterns, so each of the roughly one hundred rooms differs yet belongs to one family. | A library of 8–12 square pattern tiles in two blues on white (≈ #1F4E9A, #7FA7D9 on #F7F6F2), drawn so any tile meets any other at the edges in any rotation; used for hospitality and brand backgrounds, seeded per room/user so each surface is unique. Tile size aligns to the layout grid. |
| G4 · Perforated, lightened facades | Late buildings dissolve the wall into a screen of openings — the pierced concrete "sail" of Taranto Cathedral (1964–70), the faceted, window-punched skin of the Denver Art Museum (early 1970s). | Header bands or panels perforated with a regular grid of small cut shapes (e.g. 8px diamonds at 24px pitch, 30–40% open) through which a colour or image shows. Use for one structural band per page, never behind body text. |
| G5 · Decoration on a plain body | As art director at Richard-Ginori (from 1923) he put drawn classical figures on industrially made porcelain; from 1940 Piero Fornasetti printed surfaces for his furniture. The form stays simple; the surface sings. | Components keep plain geometry; ornament lives only on a designated "surface" layer (card back, inset panel, packaging face) as a printed illustration. Ornament never changes a component's size, shape or hit area. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** Ponti blue ≈ #1F4E9A, tile white ≈ #F7F6F2, lemon ≈ #F2D24B, sea green ≈ #2E7D6B, ash wood ≈ #CDB892, black lacquer #1B1B1B (approximate).
- **Type:** his hand-lettered captions and *Domus* covers varied widely → free: Bodoni Moda for Italian elegance, Jost (light weights) for airy captions.
- **Form:** tapered legs, faceted hexagons, thin planes, perforated screens, hand-drawn figures on smooth surfaces.
- **Composition:** symmetry relieved by lightness — thin elements floating on large clear fields; buildings and objects designed down to the furniture and fittings (Montecatini offices, Milan, 1935–38).

## Attitude

Ponti thought design should make ordinary life lighter and more joyful, and that Italian industry could carry elegance to everyone. He founded *Domus* in 1928 and edited it for most of his life, helped create the Compasso d'Oro award (1954), and taught at the Milan Polytechnic — design as culture, not just product.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `olivetti`, `achille-castiglioni` — Italian industry with wit, culture and lightness.
- **Productive tension with:** `brutalism` — perforated, lightened concrete as a bridge: heavy material made airy, mass with openings.
- **Clashes with:** `neo-brutalism-web` — thick borders and heavy offset shadows contradict a style whose whole point is visual weightlessness.

## Don't copy

The Superleggera (699) chair, the Pirelli Tower silhouette, the Parco dei Principi tile patterns, the Richard-Ginori porcelain designs, the La Pavoni "La Cornuta" espresso machine (1948), Fornasetti collaborations and the *Domus* masthead. Take weight-as-target, the closed form and the tile system.
