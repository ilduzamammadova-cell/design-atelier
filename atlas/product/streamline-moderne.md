# Streamline Moderne

**Essence:** Depression-era styling that wrapped machines in teardrop shells and speed lines to sell the feeling of moving forward.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · The shell hides the mechanism | Designers such as Raymond Loewy, Norman Bel Geddes, Henry Dreyfuss and Walter Dorwin Teague encased messy machinery (duplicators, refrigerators, locomotives) in continuous sheet-metal or Bakelite housings. Simplicity was presented, not necessarily built. | **Product:** a single seamless housing over a modular interior; service panels hidden. **UI:** one clean facade component (a single primary surface with one CTA) over complex workflows; progressive disclosure reveals the machinery only in an "advanced" layer. |
| G2 · Speed lines | Three parallel chrome bands, horizontal grooves and wraparound stripes implied motion on objects that never moved (radios, pencil sharpeners, toasters). | **Product:** parallel linear accents that run in the direction of use. **UI:** a triple-rule motif (three 1px lines, 3px apart) as a section divider or loading indicator; horizontal motion cues on progress — a stripe that travels left-to-right during async work. Use once per screen. |
| G3 · Teardrop and radius | The teardrop was treated as the ideal aerodynamic form; corners became large radii; ends were tapered. | **Product:** one large radius on leading edges, tighter radius on trailing edges. **UI:** asymmetric corner radii — e.g. 24px on the leading corners of a primary card, 4px trailing — to give buttons and cards a sense of forward direction; pill shapes for primary actions. |
| G4 · Horizontal emphasis | Long, low proportions — wraparound windows in architecture, horizontal bands on appliances — make even static objects feel stretched by speed. | **Product:** proportions wider than tall; banding parallel to the ground. **UI:** wide horizontal compositions: full-bleed bands, horizontal scroll rails, typography with slight positive tracking in uppercase labels (+4%) for a stretched feel. |
| G5 · Styling as a sales instrument | Streamlining was openly used to make products look newer and boost sales in a depressed market; restyling became an annual commercial cycle. | **Product:** know the difference between functional and promotional form; annotate which is which. **UI:** keep a "brand layer" (motion, gradients, illustrations) separate in tokens from the "function layer" so seasonal restyles never touch usability. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** polished chrome and aluminium (#D4D7DA), cream enamel (#F3EAD3), Bakelite browns and maroon (#5B2A22), jade green (#3E8E7E), railway two-tones (dark green/grey, Tuscan red).
- **Type:** chrome script logos, wide geometric sans, condensed caps on signage. Free equivalents: Poiret One, Josefin Sans, Righteous for display; Bebas Neue for condensed signage.
- **Form:** teardrops, bullet noses, fender skirts, rounded corners, porthole windows, triple speed lines, glass block.
- **Composition:** horizontal banding, symmetrical fronts, forms tapering toward the "rear".

## Attitude

Streamlining sold optimism — the promise that technology would pull America out of the Depression. It was candid about being commercial styling, and it established industrial design as a consultancy profession serving manufacturers.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `raymond-loewy`, `art-deco` — same commercial optimism and polish.
- **Productive tension with:** `dieter-rams` — streamlining's promotional surface against Rams' honesty can yield products that are calm but still feel kinetic.
- **Clashes with:** `enzo-mari` — Mari's anti-consumerist rationalism rejects form designed to accelerate obsolescence.

## Don't copy

Specific locomotive liveries (e.g. Pennsylvania Railroad S1, the 1938 20th Century Limited), the Coldspot refrigerator front, Chrysler Airflow body, Futurama exhibit imagery. Take the speed-line and asymmetric-radius logic.
