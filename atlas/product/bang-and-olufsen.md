# Bang & Olufsen

**Essence:** Technology as choreography, sculpted aluminium and glass objects that hide their controls and perform their own opening.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Hidden until approached | The Beosound Ouverture (1991, David Lewis) has two glass doors that slide apart when a hand approaches, sensed by infrared, and close behind it. The object waits, then responds to presence. | **Product:** proximity-triggered reveal; controls invisible at rest. **UI:** controls that fade in on pointer proximity or focus (opacity 0 → 1 over 160 ms when the cursor enters a 48px zone; always visible on keyboard focus and touch); idle screens show only content and time. |
| G2 · Eased mechanical motion | The Ouverture's doors move slowly, accelerate, then slow again on a mechanical ellipse, motion designed as a gesture, not just a function. | **Product:** motorised movement with authored easing curves. **UI:** an explicit motion signature: ease-in-out with a long tail (cubic-bezier(0.65, 0, 0.35, 1), 420 ms) for every reveal; reveals feel like doors opening, not windows popping. |
| G3 · Frequent controls on top, rare controls hidden | Jacob Jensen's Beomaster 1900 receiver (1976) put everyday touch controls on the surface and tucked rarely used settings under a hinged lid. | **Product:** two control tiers physically separated. **UI:** a two-tier control layout: 3 to 5 primary controls always visible; secondary settings behind a single "lid" (disclosure panel) that slides down from the same surface, not a separate page. |
| G4 · Thin horizontal slab | Jensen's Beogram 4000 turntable (1972) and receivers were extremely low, long slabs of aluminium and wood, with linear tracking and touch strips. | **Product:** long, low proportions; linear controls aligned to the slab. **UI:** horizontal control bars with linear sliders and touch strips; a "deck" layout where all transport controls sit on one slim line (height 48px) spanning the content width. |
| G5 · Material honesty in premium metal | Anodised aluminium, glass, fabric and wood are used with machined precision; the BeoLab 5 speaker (2002) adds an acoustic lens that rises from the top. | **Product:** two or three premium materials, each finished perfectly. **UI:** a three-material token set (metal = cool grey surface with fine linear gradient, glass = translucent panel, fabric = matte textured neutral) applied consistently by component role. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** natural anodised aluminium (#C7C9CC), black (#121212), smoked glass (#2B2E33 at 70%), oak (#C9A57A), occasional coloured anodising (gold tone #C6A15B, blue #2E4A6B).
- **Type:** B&O's own wordmark; clean geometric sans for labels. Free equivalents: Manrope, Urbanist, Red Hat Display.
- **Form:** thin slabs, cones and columns, motorised elements, perforated aluminium grilles, touch surfaces.
- **Composition:** sculptural single objects in a room; horizontal stretch; controls barely visible.

## Attitude

B&O treats audio and video equipment as furniture and sculpture for the home, choosing external designers (Jacob Jensen, David Lewis, later others) and giving them wide freedom. The aim is that technology should be emotionally engaging and visually calm, revealing complexity only on request.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `dieter-rams`, `jonathan-ive`, `richard-sapper`.
- **Productive tension with:** `teenage-engineering`, B&O's hidden luxury against TE's exposed instrument logic yields devices that hide everything at rest but play like instruments when opened.
- **Clashes with:** `enzo-mari`, choreographed luxury versus open, self-built honesty.

## Don't copy

The Beogram 4000, Beosound 9000 (six vertical CDs), BeoLab 5 cone, Beosound Ouverture door layout and the B&O wordmark. Take proximity reveal and two-tier controls.
