# Raymond Loewy

**Essence:** Design as persuasion, the art of making the new feel desirable but never alien.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · MAYA: Most Advanced Yet Acceptable | Loewy's rule: push novelty to the edge of what the public will accept, then stop. Familiar cues carry the unfamiliar ones. | **Product:** each release changes one big thing and keeps the rest recognisable. **UI:** when introducing a new interaction (gesture, AI panel, spatial view), wrap it in a familiar container, a standard button that launches it, a known icon, a classic list fallback; measure "acceptable" with first-use task success above 80%. |
| G2 · Clean the housing first | His first commission, the Gestetner duplicator (1929), was a redesign of the casing: a smooth shell over the same mechanism, with protruding legs removed. Perceived quality changed before engineering did. | **Product:** rationalise the enclosure, hide fasteners, reduce visible part lines. **UI:** a "facelift budget" approach, before rebuilding flows, remove visual noise: align to one grid, reduce colours to a palette of five, delete borders, unify icon set; measure perceived quality before changing logic. |
| G3 · Identity across every touchpoint | Loewy's office designed the product, the logo, the livery and the pack: Lucky Strike (1940), Exxon (1966), Shell (1971), Studebaker cars, the Air Force One livery (1962). | **Product:** one designer owns object, packaging and wordmark together. **UI:** brand tokens shared by app icon, splash, onboarding, transactional email and invoice PDF; audit that every touchpoint uses the same three hero colours and one display face. |
| G4 · Symmetry of the pack | For Lucky Strike he put the target logo on both faces of the pack, so it always sells face-up, and switched the ground from green to white. | **Product:** make the brand legible from every resting orientation. **UI:** brand and primary state readable in every context, collapsed sidebar, mobile tab bar, notification, favicon; design the 16px version first. |
| G5 · Sales curve as a design input | Loewy openly tied form to sales, arguing that ugliness does not sell; he tracked how styling changes affected market share. | **Product:** treat desirability as a measured outcome, not taste. **UI:** run controlled visual experiments (palette, imagery, hero layout) with conversion and retention metrics; keep a log of what lifted and what was neutral. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** brand-strong primaries, Lucky Strike red (#C8102E) on white, Shell yellow (#FBCE07) and red (#DD1D21), Air Force One cyan-blue (#3A8DC5) and white; chrome on transport.
- **Type:** custom wordmarks, often rounded or speed-inflected; for his register use Josefin Sans or Righteous for display, Barlow for text.
- **Form:** streamlined shells, bullet noses, wraparound windows (Greyhound Scenicruiser), sharp-edged wedge (Studebaker Avanti, 1962).
- **Composition:** strong central emblem, symmetry, generous margins, horizontal sweep on vehicles.

## Attitude

Loewy saw the industrial designer as a business partner who raises sales by raising desirability. He believed public taste moves gradually, and the designer's craft is calibrating how far ahead to lead it.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `streamline-moderne`, `corporate-identity`, commercial optimism and system-wide branding.
- **Productive tension with:** `kenya-hara`, Loewy's persuasive surface against Hara's emptiness produces brands that sell by restraint.
- **Clashes with:** `italian-radical-design`, the radicals attacked exactly the consumer-desire machine Loewy perfected.

## Don't copy

The Lucky Strike target, Shell pecten (1971), Exxon double-X, Air Force One livery, Studebaker body forms, the Greyhound running-dog styling. Take MAYA and the touchpoint-ownership idea.
