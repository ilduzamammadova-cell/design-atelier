# Isometric Illustration

**Essence:** Tidy 30°-axis drawings of miniature offices, server stacks, phones and tiny people, explaining a product as a diorama of connected blocks.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Explain systems spatially | Axonometric views show how parts connect without perspective distortion. | Use real architecture diagrams of the product — flat, labeled, arrows for data flow — in the product's own icon language; keep 3D projection for genuinely spatial things (warehouses, devices). |
| G2 · Comparable scale | With no vanishing point, everything stays measurable and comparable. | For comparisons, use flat charts and scale-true drawings: a dimension line, a ruler, a real unit. |
| G3 · Modular scenes | Isometric tiles combine like building blocks. | Build diagrams from a small kit (6–10 node shapes, 2 connector styles) on a fixed grid so every new diagram stays consistent. |

## Why it reads as slop now

Axonometric drawing is old: El Lissitzky's Proun works (c. 1919–1923), Theo van Doesburg and Cornelis van Eesteren's 1923 axonometric house studies, technical drafting, isometric video games. Around 2016–2020 it became the default way to explain cloud, fintech and logistics products; stock libraries and Dribbble packs made it cheap, and image generators now produce "isometric illustration of a data center" on request. It has largely given way to 3D renders and flat illustration, so it dates a site to roughly 2019. The cheap version: a blue-purple isometric phone, server stack and floating charts joined by dotted lines, populated with faceless mini-people.

## Subvert

- **The real diagram.** An honest technical diagram — flat, labeled, like a transit map — explains better and looks more confident.
- **Axonometric with intent.** If projection is used, make it architectural and exact — thin black lines, no gradients — closer to `de-stijl` drawings than to stock.
- **A different projection.** Oblique (cabinet) projection or exploded views with leader lines, borrowed from product manuals.

## Visual facts

- **Color:** blues and purples (#4F46E5, #60A5FA), soft gradients on faces, pastel accents.
- **Type:** paired with geometric sans; rarely any labels inside the drawing.
- **Form:** 30° axes (2:1 pixel ratio), extruded blocks, tiny figures, dashed connectors.
- **Composition:** central diorama floating on white, text around it.

## Attitude

Complex systems made tidy and miniature. Reassuring, but tidiness substitutes for explanation.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `corporate-memphis-illustration`, `purple-blue-gradient-saas`.
- **Productive tension with:** `theo-van-doesburg`, `edward-tufte` — projection and diagram used for precise explanation.
- **Clashes with:** `gradient-mesh-and-aurora` — rigid projection floating on formless backgrounds has no ground.

## Don't copy

Stock isometric packs used as brand illustration; El Lissitzky's Proun compositions; van Doesburg's specific axonometrics.

## Study

- Theo van Doesburg and Cornelis van Eesteren, axonometric studies (1923) — Het Nieuwe Instituut and MoMA collections.
- El Lissitzky, Proun series (c. 1919–1923) — MoMA, Van Abbemuseum.
- Edward Tufte, *Envisioning Information* (1990) — explaining systems on a flat page.
