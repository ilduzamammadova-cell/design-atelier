# Oversized Grotesk Hero

**Essence:** A viewport-wide headline in a tight, heavy grotesque — often all caps, negatively tracked, edge-to-edge and cropped — as the entire hero. Type as image.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · One message, maximum scale | A single statement at huge size forces focus and hierarchy. | Scale follows content length: ≤3 words may take `clamp(64px, 12vw, 200px)`; longer messages drop to 48–64px. Never shorten copy to fit the big-type look. |
| G2 · Type as image | Letterforms carry the visual; no photo or illustration needed. | Choose a face whose shapes are worth seeing large (distinctive terminals, ink traps, width contrast) and justify it from the brand idea, not the trend list. |
| G3 · Extreme scale contrast | A gigantic headline against tiny meta text creates drama. | Fix a deliberate ratio in the type scale tokens (e.g. 1:8 between 12px caption and 96px display) and use it on every page, not only the hero. |

## Why it reads as slop now

Swiss posters and Paula Scher-style typographic posters migrated to screens once variable fonts and `vw` units made fluid giant type easy (late 2010s). Agency portfolios adopted full-bleed grotesk headlines with split-text animation; template marketplaces and Framer templates copied them; by the mid-2020s SaaS brand pages used the same move. Commercial neo-grotesk releases and free lookalikes (Inter Display, Archivo, Space Grotesk) made it cheap. The cheap version: "WE BUILD DIGITAL EXPERIENCES" in 180px tight caps, letters staggering in on load, a small "(Scroll)" label, a cursor blob.

## Subvert

- **Big, but a different voice.** Giant type in a serif, a script, a mono or a wide display face — or invert the hierarchy: headline small, image huge.
- **Big type that says something specific.** A number, a name, a date, a real quote; scale earns its place when the content is a fact, not a slogan.
- **Let the grid carry scale.** Size headlines to grid units (exactly six columns wide), so scale is structural as in `josef-muller-brockmann`'s posters, not an effect.

## Visual facts

- **Color:** mostly black/white or one flat background color; occasionally one acid accent.
- **Type:** neo-grotesques and grotesques (Helvetica Now, Neue Montreal, Suisse Int'l, Aeonik, Inter Display, Archivo), tracking −3% to −6%, leading 0.85–0.95.
- **Form:** edge-to-edge crops, split lines, outlined-text variants, parenthesised labels.
- **Composition:** headline fills the width; tiny meta text in corners; lots of empty space.

## Attitude

Confidence through scale: say it big and it must be true. Effective once; across thousands of sites, volume replaces argument.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `kinetic-type-everywhere`, `marquee-tickers`, `cursor-followers-and-magnetic-buttons` — the full agency-portfolio package.
- **Productive tension with:** `editorial-serif-revival`, `wim-crouwel` — a literary or constructed voice at the same scale.
- **Clashes with:** `corporate-memphis-illustration` — a typographic manifesto turns twee beside cartoon figures.

## Don't copy

Commercial typefaces used without a license; identifiable agency homepages; Paula Scher's Public Theater posters.

## Study

- Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981) — scale from structure.
- Paula Scher's identity for The Public Theater (Pentagram, from 1994) — typographic scale with a message.
