# Ukiyo-e (Hokusai, Hiroshige)

**Essence:** Flat color, one confident keyline and audacious cropping, made as cheap popular prints for city people who wanted pleasure, travel and weather on paper.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Giant foreground, small subject | Hiroshige puts an enormous near object (a branch, a horse's leg, a lantern) against the frame edge and the real subject far behind it; depth comes from a scale jump, not perspective lines | Hero layouts where one oversized element bleeds off the viewport (at least 30% cropped) and the actual content sits small in the middle distance; never show the whole foreground object |
| G2 · Keyline over flat fields | A key block prints one dark contour of near-constant width; separate blocks lay down flat colors that may sit slightly off the line | Illustrations and spot icons built as two layers: one stroke layer (single weight, e.g. 1.5px, one ink color) above flat fill shapes nudged 1–2px out of register; no shading |
| G3 · Bokashi, the only gradient | Printers wiped the block by hand so color fades into paper at the top of a sky or the edge of water | Allow gradients only as a single-hue band on one edge of a surface (top 15–25% of a hero, fading into the paper color); never multi-hue, never radial |
| G4 · One subject, many conditions | Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji and Fifty-three Stations hold a constant motif while season, hour and weather change | Illustration systems with a fixed motif and palette where only time of day, season or weather varies; onboarding and empty states numbered as a series ("4 of 12") |
| G5 · Cartouche label | Titles sit in small colored rectangles set into a corner of the image, often vertical | Labels as solid tags pinned to an image corner and aligned to the image edge, not the page grid; vertical section labels on wide screens |
| G6 · Weather drawn as line | Rain is straight parallel hatching, snow is dots, wind is bent grass | Express system states with line textures instead of spinners: diagonal hatch for pending, dot field for inactive, bent strokes for syncing |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** imported Prussian blue (newly affordable in the late 1820s) #24406E, indigo #2B4C7E, vermilion #D9472B, ochre #C9A46A, celadon #8DA88A, sumi black #1A1A1A, paper #F1E8D6.
- **Type:** brush calligraphy in cartouches, no typefaces. For UI: a mincho or brush display face used only inside tags (Shippori Mincho, Zen Antique) with a neutral sans for running text.
- **Form:** constant-width carved contours, visible woodgrain in large flats, patterned textiles, waves with claw-like crests, stylized clouds as bands.
- **Composition:** high or tilted horizons, strong diagonals, asymmetric placement, large quiet skies, objects cut by the frame.

## Attitude

Ukiyo-e was commercial: a publisher, a designer, a carver and a printer collaborated to sell affordable images of actors, courtesans, landscapes and travel. Exquisite craft served popular pleasure. When the prints reached Europe they taught Impressionists, Toulouse-Lautrec and Art Nouveau flatness, cropping and asymmetry. The lesson for designers: mass production and refinement are not opposites.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `art-nouveau`, `japanese-modernism`, `henri-matisse` — all built on flat color, contour and asymmetric cropping inherited from these prints.
- **Productive tension with:** `swiss-international` — a strict column grid disciplines the wild cropping; the result is editorial layouts that feel calm yet cinematic.
- **Clashes with:** `op-art` — retinal vibration fights the quiet flats and the single keyline; both lose.

## Don't copy

Hokusai's Under the Wave off Kanagawa (the Great Wave) and its wave silhouette; Fine Wind, Clear Morning (Red Fuji); Hiroshige's Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Plum Park in Kameido; publisher seals and specific cartouche calligraphy. Take the cropping and the printing logic, never the images.

## Study

- Hokusai, Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (c. 1830–32); Hokusai Manga (from 1814)
- Hiroshige, Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (1833–34); One Hundred Famous Views of Edo (1856–58)
- Museum of Fine Arts, Boston — one of the largest Japanese print collections outside Japan (collections.mfa.org)
- The Met, Heilbrunn Timeline essays on ukiyo-e (metmuseum.org/toah); British Museum Japanese prints
- Meggs' History of Graphic Design, ch. 11, section on the influence of ukiyo-e
