# Op Art (Bridget Riley, Victor Vasarely)

**Essence:** Strict geometric units, gradually distorted, so that the eye itself produces motion, depth and flicker.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Perception is the subject | The viewer's own visual response — vibration, afterimage, swelling — is the work | Use in small, deliberate doses (a motion header, a launch screen), never behind text; honour prefers-reduced-motion and avoid high-frequency flicker that can trigger photosensitivity |
| G2 · Progressive distortion | A regular grid compressed or bent step by step creates a bulge or wave (Riley, Movement in Squares, 1961; Vasarely, Vega series) | Grid patterns whose cell size follows an easing curve along one axis to suggest volume; scroll-driven warping of a dot or line field |
| G3 · Black and white at full contrast | Riley's early works use only black and white | A monochrome expressive layer (pattern carries the brand) while UI chrome stays plain and neutral |
| G4 · The plastic unit | Vasarely's square-plus-inner-shape unit in contrasting colors, combinable like an alphabet | A tokenised tile (square + inner circle or rhombus) with swappable color pairs, generating endless covers, avatars and campaign art |
| G5 · Volume from line weight | Parallel lines of varying weight read as folded or impossible solids — as in the Renault diamond Vasarely and his son Yvaral drew in 1972 | Logos and icons where only stroke weight builds depth: no gradients, no shading |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** black #000000 and white #FFFFFF; Vasarely's violet #5D2E8C, cerulean #2E86C1, magenta #C2185B, acid green #7CB342, orange #F39C12; Riley's later stripes in turquoise, pink and ochre.
- **Type:** 1960s geometric sans (Questrial, Space Grotesk); type must sit on calm ground, never on pattern.
- **Form:** stripes, checkerboards, circles, rhombuses, waves, hexagonal cubes.
- **Composition:** allover, edge to edge, a uniform field with one zone of distortion.

## Attitude

Vasarely wanted a reproducible, almost industrial art built from units anyone could manufacture; Riley rejected the "op" label and spoke of sensation rather than science. MoMA's "The Responsive Eye" (1965) made the style a sensation, and fashion copied it within months — a warning about how quickly perceptual tricks become wallpaper.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `josef-albers`, `swiss-international`, `psychedelic` — systematic perception and pattern.
- **Productive tension with:** `ukiyo-e` — mechanical waves against organic carved waves; new line patterns with a hand-cut feel.
- **Clashes with:** `mark-rothko` — optical aggression destroys soft immersion.

## Don't copy

Riley's Movement in Squares (1961), Fall (1963), Current (1964); Vasarely's Zebra (1937–38), Vega-Nor (1969); the 1972 Renault diamond.

## Study

- MoMA, "The Responsive Eye" (1965, curated by William C. Seitz) catalogue and archive
- Tate (Riley, Fall); Arts Council Collection (Movement in Squares)
- Fondation Vasarely, Aix-en-Provence; Vasarely Museum, Budapest
- Bridget Riley, The Eye's Mind: Collected Writings (1999)
