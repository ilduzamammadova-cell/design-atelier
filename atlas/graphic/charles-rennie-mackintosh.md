# Charles Rennie Mackintosh

**Essence:** Art Nouveau stretched upward and straightened. Tall, thin rectangles are softened at their ends by a curve, a few small squares of colour punctuate white rooms, and the lettering is as architectural as the buildings.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · The rising vertical | His main theme is tall, thin rectangles and rising lines, with subtle curves where verticals meet horizontals, and right angles set against circles and ovals. | Tall components: card ratios of 1:2 to 1:3, vertical dividers that end in a small arc or circle, and a tall narrow image column next to text. Pair one circle (avatar, badge) with rectilinear structure for counterpoint. |
| G2 · White room, few placed objects | With Margaret Macdonald he designed light-filled white interiors with a few carefully placed pieces, unlike the cluttered rooms of the day. | Light, sparse layout: near-white surface (#F7F5EF), a few objects per view, wide spacing (section gap ≥ 96px). Ornament limited to small inset accents (see G4). |
| G3 · Architectural lettering | His lettering uses square and vertical formats and unusually high crossbars on E, F, R and H. Words lock into blocks with small squares filling gaps. | Custom display lettering with crossbars at about 70% of cap height, narrow proportions, and text set as a justified block where leftover cells are filled with small squares. For wordmarks and signage, never body text. |
| G4 · The small-square motif | Grids of tiny squares (often 3×3) and inset coloured squares recur in furniture, light fittings and graphics as rhythm marks. | A micro-motif token: a 3×3 grid of 3px squares with 2px gaps, used as bullet, separator, loading indicator and favicon detail. One motif, many small jobs. |
| G5 · Every detail of the whole | For buildings and tea rooms he designed furniture, light fittings, menus and cutlery so every detail fitted the whole. | End-to-end consistency: physical touchpoints (menus, signage, receipts, packaging) generated from the same tokens as the app, checked in one review. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** white #F7F5EF, black #1A1A1A, silver-grey #B9B6AE, lilac/purple #8E6C8A, rose pink #D79AA6, leaf green #6F8F5E.
- **Type:** hand-drawn, tall, square-ish capitals with raised crossbars. Free stand-ins: Smythe or Federant for flavour; better to draw a custom set on a vertical grid.
- **Form:** elongated stylized figures (the Four's posters), abstracted roses of concentric curves, ladder and lattice motifs, small squares.
- **Composition:** strong verticals, symmetrical towering posters (*The Scottish Musical Review*, 1896, about 2.5m tall), rectangles tempered by curves.

## Attitude

Mackintosh and the Glasgow Four (with Margaret and Frances Macdonald and Herbert McNair) took the floral style toward geometry and spirituality. They were mocked in Britain and celebrated in Vienna. Mackintosh believed in design as total environment, built from structure, light and a few meaningful details.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `vienna-secession`: a direct influence; Vienna adopted Glasgow's geometric turn.
- **Productive tension with:** `chinese-calligraphy-and-seal`: vertical columns, white space and a single red accent square share a language that could produce calm, vertical-reading layouts.
- **Clashes with:** `victorian-wood-type`: crowded horizontal shouting against poised vertical calm.

## Don't copy

- The Glasgow rose motif as drawn by Mackintosh; his high-back chair silhouettes; *Scottish Musical Review* poster; Willow Tea Rooms designs.
- Commercial "Mackintosh" fonts drawn from his lettering.
