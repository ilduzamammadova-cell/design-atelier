# The Beggarstaffs

**Essence:** Say it with what you leave out — a few flat shapes cut with scissors, a silhouette the viewer finishes, and a name in plain capitals.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Scissor-drawn edges | Two painters, brothers-in-law Pryde and Nicholson, composed by cutting coloured paper and sliding the pieces around a board before pasting — an early use of what would later be called collage. Edges are decisive and slightly irregular, never traced. | Illustrations built only from flat filled shapes: no strokes, no gradients, no outlines, at most three fills plus the ground. Edges keep a slight hand-cut irregularity (redraw the vector by eye; don't use perfect boolean primitives). |
| G2 · The viewer closes the figure | In the *Harper's Magazine* poster (1895) parts of the figure are simply missing; the eye fills the gaps and becomes complicit. | Silhouettes where one side merges with the background by sharing its exact hex, so only the lit edge is drawn. In empty states and hero art, imply 20–40% of the outline through the ground rather than drawing it. |
| G3 · The substrate is a colour | *Hamlet* (1894) was partly stencilled on brown wrapping paper; the cheap paper became one of the inks. | A mid-tone kraft surface (≈ #B08A5A) as the page colour in campaign or packaging-style UI; shapes are "cut out of" it in black and off-white, and text sits in those two inks. No pure-white page. |
| G4 · Two inks and one silhouette | The *Kassama Corn Flour* poster (1894) uses only black and yellow: one big figure, one block of lettering. | Campaign assets: two inks plus the substrate, one dominant silhouette filling 60–70% of the frame, headline in heavy plain capitals sharing an ink with the figure. If a third colour is needed, the idea isn't reduced enough. |
| G5 · Image with a slot for the name | Their unused design later called *The Coachman* (1896) was meant to become an advertisement simply by adding any client's name. | Hero illustrations designed with a reserved, fixed-size type slot (e.g. the bottom 25% band) so one image serves many products or locales; the slot never overlaps the silhouette and text is swapped from content, not redrawn. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** black #141414, kraft brown ≈ #A9824F, ochre-yellow ≈ #E3B23C, brick red ≈ #A33A2A, off-white ≈ #EDE4D0 (approximate; few inks per poster).
- **Type:** hand-drawn plain block capitals, not Art Nouveau lettering → free: Archivo Black for clean blocks, Londrina Solid for a hand-cut irregularity.
- **Form:** flat silhouettes, cast-shadow shapes, almost no interior modelling; figures cropped by the sheet edge.
- **Composition:** one figure against one empty field; lettering as a separate band at top or bottom.

## Attitude

They were academic painters who wanted posters to work at street distance and refused the floral fashion of the 1890s. Their method was reduction by trial: move the pieces until nothing more can be removed. It made them an artistic success and a commercial failure — only about a dozen designs were printed — yet the idea of the flat, object-led poster passed straight to Hohlwein and the Berlin *Sachplakat*.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `plakatstil`, `saul-bass` — flat, cut-edge silhouettes and one-idea posters.
- **Productive tension with:** `swiss-international` — hand-cut silhouettes placed on a modular grid with an objective sans keeps the warmth but gains a system.
- **Clashes with:** `owen-jones` — pattern on every surface defeats a style built on omission.

## Don't copy

*Hamlet* (1894), *Kassama Corn Flour* (1894), *Harper's Magazine* (1895), *Cinderella* (1895), *Rowntree's Elect Cocoa* (c. 1896), the Irving *Don Quixote* design, *The Coachman*; Nicholson's later woodcut books *An Alphabet* and *London Types* (both 1898).
