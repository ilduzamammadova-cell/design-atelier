# Vienna Secession

**Essence:** Ornament that became geometry: squares, checkerboards and stylized letters fitted into blocks. Its magazine was run as a design laboratory, and its workshop gave every product, form and envelope one identity.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · The square as master unit | *Ver Sacrum* (1898 to 1903) used a near-square format. Rudolf von Larisch taught filling a square with letters to sharpen figure and ground, and the Werkstätte mark sits in a square. | Square-first layout: 1:1 cards, tiles and avatars, and wordmarks drawn to fill a square block. Grid modules are square (e.g. 8×8 base, 96×96 tiles), and every logo lockup has a square variant. |
| G2 · Checker and grid ornament | Hoffmann's and Moser's patterns use black-and-white squares, grids and repeated rectangles in sequence, geometry with an organic softness. | Pattern tokens from squares only: checker strip (8px squares), dotted grid, stacked rectangles. Used as borders on tables, banners and packaging edges, always black/white plus one colour. |
| G3 · Lettering fitted to the block | Secession posters reshape letters (rounded rectangles, stretched stems) so text locks into a block, sometimes at the cost of legibility (Roller, 1902). | Display-only lockups where letters are redrawn to fill a rectangle or square, for posters, covers and event identities. Hard rule: never in navigation, forms or body. Pair with a highly legible text face. |
| G4 · Magazine as laboratory | *Ver Sacrum* had a rotating artist committee and unusual production: metallic inks, translucent papers, white-on-white embossing. Advertisers had to commission their ads from the contributors so the issue stayed unified. | Sponsored and third-party content must use the house template (ads are designed inside the system, never pasted in). Reserve an "experiment" slot per release for tactile effects such as subtle emboss (inset shadow ≤ 1px) or metallic-gradient foil on premium moments. |
| G5 · One identity across every object | Hoffmann and Moser applied the WW monogram and stylized lettering to stationery, packaging and products: an early complete house identity. | Brand application matrix: logo, type and pattern rules specified for every touchpoint (UI, email, invoice, bag, signage) in one table, with a sample for each cell. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** black #111111 and white #FAFAF7 checkers; gold #B8963E; Moser blue #2A4D9B; red #C2412D; olive #7A7A3A; colour used in flat, limited sets.
- **Type:** hand-drawn block lettering with square proportions; sans-serif slabs to fluid calligraphic forms. Free stand-ins: Federant for display; Josefin Sans for geometric supporting text.
- **Form:** squares, circles, checkerboards, stylized figures reduced to geometric patterns, laurel and leaf motifs flattened.
- **Composition:** tall vertical posters and square pages; strict horizontal and vertical alignment; ample margins around dense ornamental panels.

## Attitude

The young artists led by Gustav Klimt broke from the academic Künstlerhaus in 1897 to bring modern, international art to Vienna. They rejected French floral excess and moved toward controlled line, geometry and rich materials. The Wiener Werkstätte (1903) pursued quality in every craft, more luxurious than its English models, and Adolf Loos attacked its ornament from the sidelines.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `charles-rennie-mackintosh`, `peter-behrens`: Glasgow geometry in, systematic identity out.
- **Productive tension with:** `art-deco`: Secession squares with Deco glamour give luxury hospitality systems.
- **Clashes with:** `postmodern-new-wave`: deliberate disorder against quiet square order.

## Don't copy

- Klimt's and Moser's Secession exhibition posters (1st, 5th, 13th exhibitions), Roller's 14th and 16th posters, *Ver Sacrum* covers.
- The WW monogram and rose mark; Olbrich's Secession building gilded dome as an icon.
