# Photomontage

**Essence:** Cut real photographs and reassemble them so that the joins themselves make the argument.

The Berlin Dadaists (Raoul Hausmann, Hannah Höch 1889–1978, John Heartfield 1891–1968, George Grosz) called themselves *monteurs* — engineers who assemble rather than artists who paint. In the USSR, Gustav Klutsis (1895–1938), Lissitzky and Rodchenko turned the technique into a public language. Heartfield made around 200 covers and pages for the workers' weekly *AIZ* (1930–38).

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Dialectical pair (Heartfield) | A quotation or slogan set against an image that contradicts it, so the viewer finishes the argument | "Claim vs evidence" card: a quote at headline size paired with the photo or chart that tests it, both at equal weight; ideal for fact-check, review and comparison UIs |
| G2 · Seamless impossible image (Heartfield) | Retouched joins make an absurd scene look documentary (an X-ray spine of coins) | Concept images as photoreal composites with exactly one impossible element (scale, material swap); match light direction so the absurdity reads as intended |
| G3 · Visible cuts (Höch) | Ragged edges, jumps in scale and raw text fragments; meaning comes from rupture, often about gender and mass media | Collage headers with hard cut-out masks, 1–2px paper-white outlines, deliberately mismatched scales; suits culture and community products |
| G4 · Heroic montage (Klutsis) | One giant figure over a sea of tiny repeated figures, low viewpoint, red planes, for state campaigns | Campaign hero: one large cut-out figure overlapping a field of repeated small figures (crowd as pattern) with a diagonal red band carrying the slogan — collective-action and civic causes |
| G5 · Cut, don't zoom | Montage borrowed film's grammar: close-up, superimposition, repetition | Scroll stories that cut between scales (extreme close-up → wide) in 3–5 hard frames instead of one smooth zoom |
| G6 · One spot colour unifies found photos | Press photos of mixed origin printed in black plus one ink | Image pipeline: all user and press photos converted to duotone (black + one brand colour) so heterogeneous sources read as one voice |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** black-and-white halftone with one spot red, sometimes blue, on newsprint — `#111111`, `#D0021B`, `#EDE6D6`.
- **Type:** bold condensed grotesques; Fraktur used ironically by Heartfield; geometric Cyrillic capitals for Klutsis. Free: Oswald, Anton, Archivo Black, UnifrakturCook.
- **Form:** cut-out silhouettes, halftone dots, airbrushed seams, captions as part of the image.
- **Composition:** one dominant figure, violent scale changes, diagonals, text in bands.

## Attitude

The camera's authority can be turned against power — or harnessed by it. The same technique attacked the Nazis on *AIZ* covers and glorified Stalin's plans; Klutsis himself was shot in the purges of 1938. Use it with a clear conscience about what the montage is claiming.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `constructivism`, `punk-graphics` — cut-and-paste urgency.
- **Productive tension with:** `swiss-international` — an objective grid news app where exactly one montage slot carries the opinion piece.
- **Clashes with:** `corporate-identity` — glossy stock-photo blandness removes the friction the technique needs.

## Don't copy

Heartfield's *AIZ* covers (*Adolf the Superman*, 1932; *The Meaning of the Hitler Salute*, 1932; *Hurrah, the Butter Is Gone!*, 1935); Höch's *Cut with the Kitchen Knife* (1919–20) and *Da-Dandy* (1919); Hausmann's *The Art Critic*; Klutsis's *Electrification of the Entire Country* (1920) and his five-year-plan posters.
