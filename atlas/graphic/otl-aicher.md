# Otl Aicher (1922–1991)

**Essence:** A complete visual world built from a grid — pictograms, palette and type designed as one democratic, friendly system.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Pictograms on a strict grid | The Munich 1972 sport pictograms were drawn on a modular square grid using only horizontals, verticals and 45° diagonals, with consistent line weight; bodies became readable signs. | Icon family on a 24 × 24 grid; strokes only at 0°, 45° and 90°; one stroke weight (2px); limbs and joints constructed, never drawn freehand. Publish the grid with the icons. |
| G2 · Palette by exclusion | Munich used a partial spectrum — light blues, greens, orange, silver and neutrals — deliberately leaving out the red and black associated with authoritarian display. | Choose the brand palette by declaring which hues are excluded and why (e.g. "no red": errors signalled by orange + icon + text). The omission is part of the identity. |
| G3 · Closed identity system | The Lufthansa programme (1962, with Ulm colleagues) specified every application with a rigour that made it the prototype of the closed system. | Tokens and component specs centrally owned; no per-team overrides; every new surface (watch, kiosk, print) gets its spec before shipping. |
| G4 · Posterised photo on a colour field | Munich posters placed tone-separated photographs of athletes onto a wide field of one dominant palette colour. | Hero imagery posterised to 3–4 tones mapped to palette colours, set on a single flat field; no full-colour photography in brand moments. |
| G5 · Design from use | His writing (e.g. on the kitchen) starts from how an activity is actually performed and derives form from it. | Before layout, map the user's real task sequence (observe, don't assume); screen order and grouping follow that sequence, even if it breaks platform conventions. |
| G6 · Lightness as civic message | Munich was designed as the antithesis of 1936: airy, colourful, non-monumental. | Civic and public-service products use light backgrounds, open spacing and friendly colour; avoid heavy black chrome and monumental headlines. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** Munich palette — light blue ~#6EB4E6, darker blue ~#0072BC, light green ~#7CC242, dark green ~#00843D, orange ~#F28C28, silver/grey, white.
- **Type:** Univers for Munich; later his own Rotis (1988) → Hanken Grotesk (Univers-like).
- **Form:** pictograms, modular grids, posterised photography, stripes.
- **Composition:** strict publication grids, consistent positioning of symbol and text, colour as primary differentiator.

## Attitude

Co-founder of the Ulm school (1953) and deeply shaped by anti-Nazi resistance in his circle, Aicher saw design as a democratic, anti-authoritarian practice: making the world usable and transparent rather than impressive. Systems were for freedom and clarity, not control.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `swiss-international`, `corporate-identity`, `ulm-school` — rigour at the scale of whole events and companies.
- **Productive tension with:** `lance-wyman` — Aicher's neutral grid against Wyman's place-rooted pattern: systematic yet local icon systems.
- **Clashes with:** `punk-graphics` — raw, photocopied disorder dissolves the grid that carries his whole language.

## Don't copy

The Munich 1972 sport pictograms and Waldi mascot, the Munich emblem, Lufthansa's crane treatment, Rotis outlines, the ERCO pictograms.
