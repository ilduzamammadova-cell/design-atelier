# International Typographic Style (Swiss Style)

**Essence:** The page as a measured field where a grid, one grotesque typeface and a documentary photograph let information speak without persuasion.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Modular field | Space is divided into arithmetically related fields before anything is placed; type, image and diagram all snap to field edges, which is why wildly different posters still look like siblings. | Define a field grid, not just columns: e.g. 12 columns × rows of 6 baselines (6 × 24px) on desktop. Every card, image and text block spans whole fields; gutters equal one baseline (24px). Nothing is positioned by eye. |
| G2 · Flush-left grotesque | One sans-serif family, set ragged right, never justified or centred; hierarchy comes from size and weight steps only. | One family (Archivo or Schibsted Grotesk). Text left-aligned everywhere, including buttons and headings. Four sizes max (14/20, 18/24, 32/40, 64/72), two weights. No italic, colour or underline for hierarchy. |
| G3 · Objective image | Photographs are evidence, not mood: frontal, sharp, often black and white, cropped hard and scaled for impact rather than retouched for glamour. | Images are full-bleed or locked to column edges, square-cornered, no shadows or gradient overlays. Default treatment: greyscale or single-ink duotone; captions sit under images in the body size. |
| G4 · Asymmetric balance by void | Equilibrium comes from weighting one side and leaving deliberate, measured emptiness elsewhere, never from a centred axis. | Hero content occupies columns 1–7; columns 8–12 stay empty at all breakpoints above tablet. The empty area is specified in the layout, not left over. |
| G5 · Parallel-language columns | Trilingual journals and signage taught the style to carry several languages side by side in equal columns. | Localised or comparative content in equal columns of identical width; layout tested with +35% text length (German) without reflowing the grid. |
| G6 · Factual voice | Copy states facts; no superlatives, no exclamation marks; the designer is a neutral conduit. | Microcopy rules: labels are nouns or verbs, never slogans; no "!", no emoji; numbers shown with units; empty states describe the state rather than cheer. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** black #111111, white #FFFFFF, one signal red (~#E30613); occasional second primary (blue ~#0057B8). Large flat fields of a single colour.
- **Type:** Akzidenz-Grotesk, Neue Haas Grotesk/Helvetica (1957), Univers (1957) → Archivo, Schibsted Grotesk, Hanken Grotesk, Public Sans.
- **Form:** squares, circles, rules, unretouched photography; no ornament, no illustration flourishes.
- **Composition:** modular grid, asymmetric balance, generous margins, information grouped in blocks aligned on shared edges.

## Attitude

Its pioneers believed graphic design was a public service: a postwar, multilingual, increasingly global society needed messages that were clear, verifiable and free of propaganda's tricks. Order was not a look but an ethic — the designer's personality was supposed to step back so the content could be trusted.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `ulm-school`, `otl-aicher`, `massimo-vignelli` — all treat the system as the product; together they produce rigorous design systems.
- **Productive tension with:** `push-pin-studios` — warm illustration and historical lettering placed inside a strict field grid gives contemporary editorial design its "rational but human" feel.
- **Clashes with:** `psychedelic` — both want to own the whole surface, one through measured void, the other through all-over ornament; a mix usually reads as parody of one of them.

## Don't copy

Müller-Brockmann's *Der Film* (1960) and Tonhalle *Musica Viva* posters, Hofmann's *Giselle* (1959), Vivarelli's *Neue Grafik* covers, Ballmer's *Norm* (1928). Use licensed grotesques; never redraw Helvetica or Univers outlines.
