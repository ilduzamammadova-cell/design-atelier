# Japanese Postwar Graphic Modernism

**Essence:** Western constructivist geometry fused with the frontal, centred emblem of the family crest, one charged symbol in a quiet field.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · The crest logic | Postwar designers drew on the *mon*, the simplified family emblem contained in a circle: frontal, symmetrical, reduced to a few planes. | Brand marks, app icons and category badges as centred emblems inside a circle or square, frontal (never three-quarter view), symmetrical about a vertical axis, readable at 24px. |
| G2 · Central axis composition | Instead of Europe's relational asymmetry, much Japanese work organises space around a median axis. | Key brand screens (splash, onboarding, campaign pages) composed symmetrically around the vertical centre; content column centred with wide equal margins; a single focal element on the axis. |
| G3 · One element in a charged field | A single sun, fish or form floats in a large, carefully toned emptiness; the void carries meaning. | Hero with one object occupying ≤ 15% of the area on a flat or very softly graded field; no secondary decoration; text small and placed on the axis below. |
| G4 · Tradition rebuilt in planes | Ikko Tanaka reconstructed Noh faces, landscapes and woodblock motifs from flat geometric planes on a grid. | Cultural or seasonal illustrations rebuilt from 4 to 8 flat geometric planes on a visible grid; no outlines, no texture; one warm/cool contrast per image. |
| G5 · Gradation as light | Designers such as Koichi Sato used soft gradations and glows to make the printed page emit light. | One radial or linear glow gradient allowed per product, reserved for "alive" states (recording, live, AI thinking); everything else flat. |
| G6 · Mixed scripts, mixed directions | Japanese and Latin text, vertical and horizontal lines, share one composition. | Multi-script layouts support vertical CJK blocks (`writing-mode: vertical-rl`) beside horizontal Latin; align both to the same grid lines. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** vermilion sun red ~#D7261E, sumi black, paper white, indigo ~#1F3A60, close-valued blues and greens; occasional luminous gradients.
- **Type:** Mincho and Gothic Japanese faces with Western grotesques and Bodoni → Shippori Mincho, Zen Kaku Gothic New, Noto Serif/Sans JP; Bodoni Moda or Archivo for Latin.
- **Form:** circles, crests, flat planes, fine lines, photographic objects isolated on dark grounds.
- **Composition:** central axis or strong single-point focus, large voids, technical perfection of print.

## Attitude

After 1945 designers such as Yusaku Kamekura professionalised the field, founded clubs and design centres, and positioned Japan internationally through events like the 1964 Tokyo Olympics. Their aim was neither imitation of the West nor nostalgia, but a modern language with Japanese roots in which aesthetic quality strengthens the message.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `swiss-international`, `yusaku-kamekura`, `ikko-tanaka`, shared reduction and technical precision.
- **Productive tension with:** `pop-art`, the emblematic calm cracked open by mass-media collage, as in Tadanori Yokoo's 1960s work.
- **Clashes with:** `punk-graphics`, the calm, centred emblem cannot survive raw photocopied chaos.

## Don't copy

The Tokyo 1964 emblem and posters, Ikko Tanaka's *Nihon Buyo* poster (1981), Shigeo Fukuda's *Victory 1945* (1975), Yokoo's posters, and any specific *kamon*, family and institutional crests (including the imperial chrysanthemum) belong to lineages and must not be reused as decoration.
