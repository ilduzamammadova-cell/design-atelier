# Corporate Identity and Visual Systems

**Essence:** An organization speaks with one voice when every surface, from a matchbook to an aircraft, obeys the same few rules.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Redundancy becomes identity | Chermayeff & Geismar's Chase Manhattan programme (1960) showed that rigorously repeating one mark, one colour and one typeface makes consistency itself recognisable. | Three locked brand tokens, mark, one brand colour, one typeface, appear on every surface (app, email, PDF, slide, receipt). Zero local exceptions; violations are lint errors. |
| G2 · The standards manual | Lufthansa (Ulm, 1962), the NYC Transit Authority manual (1970) and the U.S. federal programmes (1970s) specified every application, from timetables to food packaging. | A living design-system site with tokens published as code, every component state specified, and paired "do / don't" examples; the manual is the source of truth, not the Figma file. |
| G3 · Abstract mark | The Chase octagon was free of letters, pictures and figurative meaning, proof that pure geometry can identify a large organisation. | Brand symbol drawn as a pure geometric form that also works as favicon, loader, map pin and wayfinding marker; specify it on a construction grid. |
| G4 · Closed vs open system | Lufthansa aimed for absolute uniformity; CBS and IBM relied on consistently high quality and gave designers latitude. | Decide explicitly and document which: closed (locked components, no overrides) or open (principles + exemplars + review). Mixed, undeclared systems decay fastest. |
| G5 · Pictograms across languages | Tokyo 1964, Mexico 1968, Munich 1972 and the U.S. DOT symbol set (AIGA, 1974) built families of signs readable without words. | Icon family on one grid with one stroke weight, one corner rule and one terminal style; test comprehension with people from at least three language backgrounds. |
| G6 · Letters for the oblique view | Chase used an extended sans because signs are often read by pedestrians at sharp angles. | Labels viewed at angle, small size or speed (kiosks, wearables, car UI) use a wide or extended sans; avoid condensed faces there. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** one ownable corporate colour per brand (IBM blue, Mobil red, Lufthansa blue/yellow) plus black, white and greys.
- **Type:** Helvetica, Univers, Akzidenz, custom extended or geometric sans → Archivo (incl. expanded widths), Hanken Grotesk.
- **Form:** geometric monograms and symbols, stripes, pictograms on modular grids.
- **Composition:** grid-based templates for every format; logo placement fixed by rule.

## Attitude

Postwar design leaders argued that good design is good business: an organisation's reputation for quality could be built, managed and protected through visual consistency. Design became a management function with executive authority, and the designer became the author of rules rather than of single artefacts.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `swiss-international`, `otl-aicher`, `massimo-vignelli`, systems thinking and grid discipline.
- **Productive tension with:** `push-pin-studios`, personality and illustration admitted into a strict system; the basis of many contemporary "flexible identity" programmes.
- **Clashes with:** `grapus`, anonymous corporate uniformity versus collective, handmade, political urgency.

## Don't copy

The CBS eye (1951), IBM (1956/1972), Chase (1960), Mobil (1964), the Lufthansa crane treatment (1962), the NASA "worm" (1975), the DOT pictograms as a set.
