# E. McKnight Kauffer (1890–1954)

**Essence:** Modern painting translated into public persuasion — each brief solved in a different idiom, landscapes reduced to interlocking planes and symbols.

An American who settled in London in 1914, Kauffer produced 141 posters for London's Underground under Frank Pick, a long series for Shell-Mex and BP under Jack Beddington, book jackets for literary publishers, and *The Art of the Poster* (1924). MoMA showed his work in 1937; he returned to the US in 1940.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · A symbol, not a depiction | He looked for a form that belonged only to the product — a flight of birds for an early-morning newspaper (*Daily Herald*, 1918–19) | Each campaign or feature gets one bespoke abstract symbol derived from its benefit, stored in a library with a one-line rationale; no stock imagery |
| G2 · Idiom follows brief | Cubist, art deco, surrealist and photomontage solutions chosen per job, without a fixed house style | Brand core fixed (type, palette, logo), but illustration idiom chosen per campaign from a curated list of 3–4 approved idioms |
| G3 · Landscape as interlocking planes | Rural destinations at the end of the lines edited into flat interlocking colour shapes | Location and map illustrations from 5–7 flat interlocking colour planes, no outlines, each plane a palette token |
| G4 · Odd format, plain ground | A tall vertical format and a strikingly empty background made the *Daily Herald* poster stand out | Non-standard aspect ratios for key assets (1:3 tall cards, 3:1 bands) on plain flat fields; the format itself is the attention device |
| G5 · Classical meets graphic | 1930s London Transport posters juxtapose a sculpted head with a flat palette or stage symbol | Culture features pair one photographic classical object with one flat graphic symbol; never two photos, never two symbols |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** muted lyrical palettes for the Underground (greens, ochres, blue-greys), bolder black/red/blue for Shell — approx. `#2F4B3A`, `#C9A34A`, `#6E8AA6`, `#D9472B`, `#121212`.
- **Type:** his own lettering, outline slabs and condensed sans; Underground posters paired with Johnston Sans. Free: Railway Sans (Johnston revival), Josefin Sans, Rokkitt.
- **Form:** silhouettes, faceted planes, airbrushed gradients, photomontage in the 1930s.
- **Composition:** tall verticals, diagonal flight lines, generous plain fields.

## Attitude

Modern art belongs on the street; the designer interprets the avant-garde for a public audience without talking down to it. His career depended on enlightened patrons (Pick, Beddington) — proof that client vision is a design material.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `a-m-cassandre`, `abram-games` — pictorial modernism carried into persuasion.
- **Productive tension with:** `new-typography` — his pictorial symbols paired with asymmetric functional text give campaign pages both image and information.
- **Clashes with:** `memphis` — busy pattern-on-pattern drowns the single symbol at the heart of his method.

## Don't copy

The *Daily Herald* "Soaring to Success" poster; his London Underground posters (e.g. *Winter Sales*, *Power*); Shell posters (*Actors Prefer Shell*, *Aeroshell*); *Contact with the World – Use the Telephone* (1934); his book jackets.
