# Ludwig Mies van der Rohe

**Essence:** A few perfect planes on a strict grid, one precious material, and all the effort spent where two things meet.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Free-standing plane | In the Barcelona Pavilion (1929, designed with Lilly Reich) walls stand as independent slabs between a floor plinth and a roof plane. They steer movement but never close a room. | No boxed cards. Content is grouped by a single surface plane (a tinted slab) that runs past the content edge on one side and stops short on the other. Groups are separated by planes and air, never by a border drawn on all four sides. |
| G2 · Universal space | Column-free halls such as Crown Hall (IIT) and the Neue Nationalgalerie make one large room that can take any use; services are pushed into cores. | The main view is one open canvas with no permanent sidebars cutting it up. Tools dock at the outer edges or appear when called, and the canvas adapts to any task without changing layout. |
| G3 · One precious material | In an otherwise bare frame, a single rich surface becomes the focus: the onyx wall at Barcelona, the Macassar ebony wall at the Tugendhat House. | A system of neutrals plus exactly one "precious" token: a deep veined image, a bronze, a dark green stone tone. It appears once per screen, on the single most important surface (the selected plan, the hero product), and never as a repeating accent. |
| G4 · Corner detail | His junctions carry the structure's logic onto the surface: cruciform chrome-clad columns at Barcelona, applied bronze I-beam mullions on the Seagram Building. | Spend the polish budget on junctions: where a sticky header meets a sticky column, where an input meets its button, where a table row meets the page edge. Shared radii, borders that meet without doubling, pixel-exact alignment. Junction specs are documented as components in their own right. |
| G5 · The plinth | Farnsworth House floats above the ground; the Neue Nationalgalerie sits on a granite podium. The building is set apart from the everyday by a raised plane. | Hero content sits on a "podium": one tonal step lighter than the page, wide margins on all sides, reached by a single step (one primary action). Nothing else on the page is raised. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** travertine (≈ #E8DFCF), green marble (≈ #3E5B4A), golden onyx (≈ #D9B98A), bronze (≈ #6B4A2F), black steel (≈ #1E1E1E), chrome, grey-green glass.
- **Type:** no signature typeface; his world pairs with a neutral grotesque → "Hanken Grotesk" or "Schibsted Grotesk", set small with generous tracking.
- **Form:** thin steel, cruciform columns, floor-to-ceiling glass, flat roof slabs, stone in book-matched panels.
- **Composition:** asymmetrical planes on a regular column grid (close to a De Stijl plan); long horizontal floor and roof planes; repeated façade bays.

## Attitude

Mies believed architecture should express the technology of its time and then be refined until only structure and proportion remain. Restraint was not emptiness for him: the craft goes into fewer, better decisions and into very expensive junctions. The open space is left for life to fill.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `dieter-rams`, `swiss-international`, fewer, better details on a strict system; they share Mies's trust in the grid.
- **Productive tension with:** `alvar-aalto`, Mies's cool, perfect frame warmed by Aalto's tactile touch points; gives a precise system where every interactive surface feels human.
- **Clashes with:** `postmodern-new-wave`, layered collage and noise fill the open space Mies keeps empty; both lose their point.

## Don't copy

The Barcelona chair and stool, the MR cantilever chairs and Brno chair (all still produced under license); the Barcelona Pavilion plan as a graphic; the Seagram façade or the Farnsworth House as imagery.
