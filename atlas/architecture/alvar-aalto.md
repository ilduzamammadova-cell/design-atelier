# Alvar Aalto

**Essence:** Modernism tuned to human nerves: industrial parts shaped for the tired, the ill and the hand, with one free curve loosening every grid.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Design for the person lying down | At Paimio Sanatorium (1929 to 33) patient rooms were planned from the bedridden patient's view: a calming dark ceiling color, lamps kept out of the eye line, quiet low-splash washbasins so a roommate is not disturbed. | Design each critical screen for the user's worst state (exhausted, one-handed, on a phone at 2 a.m.). Primary action within thumb reach, no motion on error states, sounds off by default, a dark theme tuned for low light. The spec names this "patient state" and tests it first. |
| G2 · One signature joint, many products | The bent-birch L-leg (patented 1933) joins leg to seat and recurs across Stool 60, tables and benches; later Y- and X-legs extended the same idea. | Define one distinctive connector detail (a particular corner radius plus notch, a tab shape, a bracket) and reuse it across every component family. New variants are new arrangements of the same joint, never new joints. |
| G3 · Free curve on an orthogonal frame | The undulating timber ceiling of the Viipuri Library lecture hall (for acoustics), the Savoy vase (1936), the wave-shaped Baker House dormitory (MIT, 1947 to 49), all set against straight grids. | In an otherwise rectilinear layout, allow exactly one free curve per view: a wavy section edge, a curved chart area, an organic image mask. It must do a job (guide the eye, separate two zones), never pure decoration. |
| G4 · Warm where the hand touches | At Villa Mairea (1938 to 39) steel columns are wrapped in rattan and wood where people brush past; handles are clad and shaped for the hand. | Interactive surfaces are warm and static surfaces cool: buttons, sliders and handles take warm tints (birch ≈ #D9B98A, leather ≈ #8A5A3B) and slightly softer radii; non-interactive panels stay in cool greys. Users find touch points by temperature, not only by hue. |
| G5 · Flexible standardisation | Industrially produced parts in series, combined to fit varied sites and people rather than one rigid type. | A component library of few base parts with many permitted compositions. Each component ships in 2 to 3 humane densities (compact, comfortable, large-accessible) instead of dozens of decorative style options. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** blonde birch (≈ #E3C9A0), white plaster (≈ #F4F1EA), staircase yellow at Paimio (≈ #E8B83A), soft green-grey (≈ #8C9A7E), black lacquer, copper and brass, blue glazed tile.
- **Type:** no signature typeface; Artek graphics use clean sans → free: "Source Sans 3" or "Nunito Sans".
- **Form:** bent laminated birch, wave lines, fan-shaped plans, rough brick beside fine timber, cone-shaped round skylights.
- **Composition:** splayed and fan-shaped plans that turn toward sun and site; an orthogonal core with one free-form element.

## Attitude

Aalto argued for a humanised modernism: technology and standardisation are only good when they serve human psychology, including people at their weakest. The Finnish forest was his model for variety within a system. He worked in close partnership with Aino Aalto, who co-founded Artek in 1935 and designed glassware and interiors in her own right.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `scandinavian-modern`, `frank-lloyd-wright`, natural materials, total design, human scale.
- **Productive tension with:** `brutalism`, raw mass softened only at the touch points; gives a tough system that still feels kind to the hand.
- **Clashes with:** `op-art`, optical vibration is the opposite of a calm environment designed for the vulnerable.

## Don't copy

The Paimio armchair, Stool 60 and the L-leg (Artek designs, still licensed), the Aalto/Savoy vase (Iittala), the Golden Bell pendant; Aalto's signature.
