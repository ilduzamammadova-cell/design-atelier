# Christopher Alexander

**Essence:** Good places are built from a shared language of small, linked solutions that ordinary people can use — the idea that gave software its design patterns.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · The pattern format | *A Pattern Language* (1977, with Sara Ishikawa, Murray Silverstein and others) sets out 253 numbered patterns, each stating a context, a problem and a solution, linked upward to larger patterns and downward to smaller ones. It shaped software design patterns and Ward Cunningham's first wiki. | Document the design system as a pattern language: every pattern page has a name, a number, context ("used inside…"), the problem in one sentence, the solution as one rule plus one picture, and links up and down. Patterns combine like sentences instead of sitting in a flat component inventory. |
| G2 · Intimacy gradient | Pattern 127: spaces are sequenced from the most public at the entrance to the most private at the back. | Order IA along an intimacy gradient: public pages → shared team spaces → personal workspace → settings and security. Tone deepens as you go in: warmer palette, more personal copy, quieter density. |
| G3 · Entrance transition | A graceful change of light, level, surface or view between the street and the inside helps people shift into the private world. | When users cross into a private or focused space (an editor, a workspace, a payment step), give a short threshold: a change of background tone and a brief, single transition — never a hard cut from marketing chrome into the tool. |
| G4 · Levels of scale and strong centers | In *The Nature of Order* (4 vols, 2002–05) living structure has a full range of sizes with no big jumps, and centers that strengthen each other. | Type and spacing scales with no missing steps (each about 1.5–2× the last), and every level present on a screen: detail, element, group, section, page. Each group has a visible center (a heading, an icon, an image) that its children support. |
| G5 · Roughness and not-separateness | Living structure adapts slightly to its context (roughness) and is connected to its surroundings rather than isolated. | Let local fit beat global rule: a component may stretch or shift a few pixels to align with its neighbour or its content. No element looks pasted on; each shares at least one edge, color or rhythm with what surrounds it. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** in his built work and examples: whitewash (≈ #F2EEE4), ochre (≈ #C99A4A), terracotta (≈ #A65136), deep green (≈ #2F4A3A), indigo (≈ #2C3E6B), touches of gold.
- **Type:** *A Pattern Language* uses numbered headings, a bold problem statement and a "therefore" solution, with small black-and-white photos → free: "Source Serif 4" for text, small-caps numerals for pattern numbers.
- **Form:** arcades, alcoves, window seats, ornamental borders, hand-built timber and plaster.
- **Composition:** nested centers, public-to-private gradients, courtyards, positive outdoor space.

## Attitude

Alexander believed design should produce a living quality he called the "quality without a name", and that people can shape their own environments with a shared language. He opposed top-down modernist abstraction, which made him an outsider in architecture and an unexpected hero in software.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `arts-and-crafts`, `alvar-aalto` — humane, participatory, made to fit people.
- **Productive tension with:** `swiss-international` — Swiss systematic rigour combined with local roughness and strong centers gives grids that feel lived in.
- **Clashes with:** `mies-van-der-rohe` — universal, neutral space is the opposite of specific, intimate places; Alexander argued directly against that tradition.

## Don't copy

The pattern texts, diagrams and photographs of his books (copyrighted); his pattern names and numbers presented as your own system — cite them when used; his built projects.
