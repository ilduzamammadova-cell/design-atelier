# Brutalism

**Essence:** An ethic of honesty: the structure, the material and the process of making are left visible and become the only decoration.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Process left visible | Board-marked concrete keeps the grain of the timber formwork; Paul Rudolph's Yale Art & Architecture Building (1963) has ribbed concrete hammered by hand. | Let the making show as texture: a faint 1 px construction grid stays in the background, build IDs and version stamps sit openly in the footer, skeleton states share the final layout exactly. Nothing pretends to have appeared by magic. |
| G2 · Function as legible mass | Each function gets its own expressed volume: fly towers, auditoria, stair cores and lift shafts read from outside (National Theatre, London; Boston City Hall). | Each module (navigation, content, tools, status) is a distinct block with its own weight, readable from a thumbnail: heavy filled slabs for structure, lighter fields for content. The layout reads like an exploded diagram, without needing labels. |
| G3 · Raw material as finish | The structural material is the final surface; nothing is clad or painted to look like something else. | Use the medium's own materials as the finish: system font stack, native form controls, plain 1 px borders, square corners. Restyle only to improve clarity, never to disguise. See `brutalist-web` for the full web version. |
| G4 · Monumental repetition | Precast units and deep-set window modules repeated across huge façades (Barbican Estate; Habitat 67's stacked boxes). | Long lists and grids built from one heavy unit repeated without variation; a deep inset content well with a 12–16 px frame, repeated hundreds of times. Rhythm is the ornament. |
| G5 · Streets in the sky | Park Hill, Sheffield (1957–61): access decks wide enough for neighbours to meet and milk floats to pass, a street lifted into the building. | Treat connective tissue as a place: activity feeds, comment rails and shared corridors get real width, a name and their own surface, instead of being squeezed into a drawer. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** concrete greys (≈ #B5B1A8 light, #8E8B85 mid, #5E5B56 dark), weather staining, occasional brick (≈ #8A4B36) and planting green.
- **Type:** heavy grotesques and slab serifs on signage, often cast or stencilled in concrete → free: "Archivo Black", "Roboto Slab", "Space Grotesk".
- **Form:** massive volumes, cantilevers, board-marked and bush-hammered surfaces, deep-set windows, service towers.
- **Composition:** clusters of expressed volumes on podiums and raised decks; megastructures; deep shadow as the main source of contrast.

## Attitude

The British "New Brutalism" (Alison and Peter Smithson; Reyner Banham's 1955 essay in *The Architectural Review*) was first an ethic: honesty about structure, materials and services, and an acceptance of things "as found". It was tied to post-war social housing, universities and civic buildings: tough, plain architecture for the many. Le Corbusier's béton brut gave it its name and material.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `brutalist-web`, `constructivism` — function on display, structure as image.
- **Productive tension with:** `alvar-aalto` — raw mass that turns warm only where people touch it gives an honest system with humane touch points.
- **Clashes with:** `art-nouveau` — sinuous applied ornament on raw mass reads as disguise, the one thing Brutalism forbids.

## Don't copy

Specific building silhouettes as logos or hero imagery (Trellick Tower, the Barbican towers, Habitat 67, Boston City Hall); the Barbican's own signage system; named photographers' images of these buildings.

## Study

- Reyner Banham, *The New Brutalism: Ethic or Aesthetic?* (1966).
