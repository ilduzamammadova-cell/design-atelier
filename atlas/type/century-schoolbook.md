# Century Schoolbook

**Essence:** Legibility as a commission. It is a sturdy, open, slightly wide serif made so children could learn to read from it, and it later became the voice of courts and public records.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Designed against a reading brief | Textbook publisher Ginn & Co. asked ATF for an easy-to-read schoolbook face. Morris Fuller Benton is said to have consulted contemporary legibility research, including a 1912 Clark University study that ranked Century faces among the most legible. | Choose type with the actual reader: test 5–8 users from the target group on their own devices, find the size at which misreadings start, and set the body token 1–2px above it. Document the test next to the token. |
| G2 · Open counters, larger x-height, sturdier strokes, wider spacing | Compared with its Century ancestors, Schoolbook opens counters, raises the x-height, adds stroke weight and loosens fit, all to help young eyes. | Settings for children, low-vision and older users: body ≥ 18px, weight 450–500 (variable), letter-spacing +1%, line-height 1.6, sentence case only. All-caps headings are banned. |
| G3 · The face of the record | The US Supreme Court requires briefs to be set in a Century family face at 12 point and prints its own opinions in Century Schoolbook. | "Document of record" style for exports, receipts, certificates, legal notices: Century-type text at 12pt equivalent, wide margins, numbered paragraphs, no decorative display face. |
| G4 · Letters that match handwriting | Century Schoolbook Infant, used in Eric Hill's *Spot* books, swaps in single-storey *a* and *g* to match the shapes children learn to write. | In early-reader and learning apps, enable single-storey alternates (a stylistic set such as `ss01`, or an infant cut) so screen letters match the handwriting the child is taught. |

## Visual facts

- **Classification:** sturdy American "modern" serif in the Scotch Roman tradition: vertical stress, low-to-moderate contrast.
- **Origin:** the family began with Century Roman (Linn Boyd Benton for Theodore Low De Vinne's *Century Magazine*, 1894). Morris Fuller Benton drew Century Expanded (1900) and then Century Schoolbook (1918–19) for American Type Founders.
- **Main digital versions:** Century Schoolbook (Monotype and others), New Century Schoolbook (Linotype; one of the PostScript core fonts), ITC Century (1970s), Eames Century Modern (House Industries). All commercial.
- **Anatomy:** large x-height, open round counters, moderate contrast, sturdy bracketed serifs, ball terminals, generous spacing, slightly wide set.
- **Free / open alternatives:** TeX Gyre Schola (GUST font licence; adds Cyrillic and small caps). C059 (URW++ base 35 set, AGPL with a document-embedding exception). Century Catalogue (Raph Levien, OFL; unfinished).

## Attitude

Benton treated readability as a duty to a specific reader, the child. The face carries no flourish. It believes clarity is a public good, which is why courts, schools and civil servants still choose it.

## Using it today

- **Signals:** North American schoolbooks, US Supreme Court opinions and legal briefs, children's picture books. On the web it is rare, so it reads as earnest, institutional and kind rather than trendy.
- **Robotic ↔ humane: 6/10.** Vertical stress and regular construction feel orderly. The large open counters, ball terminals and loose spacing feel patient and kind.
- **UI fitness:** body text excellent at ≥ 16px; labels possible at 14px thanks to the x-height, but the wide set costs space; display solid but plain. Sturdy hairlines survive 1× screens and dark mode. Check Cyrillic and Greek; TeX Gyre Schola covers Cyrillic.
- **Pairs with:** an American gothic sans (Franklin Gothic, or open Libre Franklin) for headings and UI. Atkinson Hyperlegible (Braille Institute, OFL) for labels in accessibility-first products. A friendly geometric sans for children's headings (open: Nunito).

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `inclusive-design`: the face is a historical case of designing for a specific, under-served reader.
- **Productive tension with:** `memphis`: schoolbook text inside loud colour and pattern makes play legible, toy-box energy with a teacher's clarity.
- **Clashes with:** `didot`: haughty hairlines and an earnest schoolbook voice have nothing to say to each other.

## Don't copy

House Industries' Eames Century Modern and other commercial outlines; the *Spot* book typography; Supreme Court document templates as brand style.
