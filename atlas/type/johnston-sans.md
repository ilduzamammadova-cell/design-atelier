# Johnston Sans (1916)

**Essence:** A city's private alphabet — the first great sans of the 20th century, reasoned from the pen and Roman capitals, used for over a century as the voice of London's transport.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Exclusive typeface as identity | Commissioned by Frank Pick and introduced on the Underground in 1916, the face belonged only to the network; recognition came from lettering before logos did that job. | Treat the house font as the primary brand asset: the wordmark is the name set in it, and no other face appears in signage, app nav or tickets. Commission 2–3 custom glyphs (or stylistic set `ss01`) to make an open face ownable. |
| G2 · Roman capitals, even stroke | Capitals keep inscriptional widths (round O, narrow E, wide M) with a plain monoline stroke — modern but rooted. | For station-style labels: caps with varied widths, +5% tracking, ≥ 16px on screen and ≥ 1/200 of viewing distance on signs; sentence case for anything over three words. |
| G3 · Diamond dots and the curled l | The i and j dots are small diamonds (a rotated square), and the lowercase l has a tail so it cannot be read as I — tiny, consistent signatures. | Pick one micro-shape (diamond, square, circle) and give it every "dot" job: tittles in a custom set, bullets, status dots, separators. Ensure Il1 are distinct in any face you adopt for codes and line names. |
| G4 · Revision without replacement | New Johnston (Eiichi Kono at Banks & Miles, 1979) added weights and italics for photo-typesetting; Johnston100 (Monotype, 2016) returned to the archive drawings, added Hairline and Thin and new symbols such as # and @. | Version your typeface like code: document what each revision changed and why (new medium, new glyphs, new weights). When a brand font is updated, re-test every component at its minimum size. |

## Visual facts

- **Classification:** humanist sans (early, pen-derived).
- **Designer · year · foundry:** Edward Johnston for the Underground Electric Railways of London, commissioned 1913, in use from 1916; Eric Gill assisted early on.
- **Digital versions & licences:** New Johnston (Eiichi Kono, Banks & Miles, 1979); Johnston100 (Malou Verlomme with Nadine Chahine, Monotype, 2016). Proprietary to Transport for London — not licensable. ITC Johnston and P22 Underground are commercial revivals (P22 under a London Transport Museum agreement).
- **Proportions:** moderate x-height, Roman capital widths, open apertures, monoline stroke, diamond tittles, curled l.
- **Free / open alternatives (keep the gene):** Railway Sans (Greg Fleming, OFL — built on Justin Howes's 1994 digitisation of Johnston's original artwork); Cabin (OFL — humanist sans inspired by Johnston and Gill, with variable widths).

## Attitude

Pick and Johnston believed a public service should speak in one clear, well-made voice — fitness for purpose as a civic courtesy. The typeface was infrastructure, maintained as carefully as the trains.

## Using it today

- **Signals:** London Underground roundels, Tube maps, TfL buses and posters; reads "London, public, trustworthy". Saturation: specific — instantly read as TfL, so close imitation borrows someone else's identity.
- **Robotic ↔ humane:** 7/10 — pen-derived shapes, Roman proportions and the friendly curled l and diamond dots; monoline stroke and a geometric O keep it disciplined.
- **UI fitness:** signage, headings, labels; text use is limited to TfL. For your product, take the genes via Railway Sans or Cabin; check language coverage (Railway Sans is Latin-focused).
- **Pairs with:** Caslon or another sturdy old-style serif for long reading; Work Sans or Akzidenz for timetables and tabular data; a pictogram set drawn at the same stroke weight as the type.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `harry-beck`, `edward-johnston`, `e-mcknight-kauffer` — the London Transport design culture.
- **Productive tension with:** `din` — pen-born humanism against engineered standard letters yields warm but technical transit UI.
- **Clashes with:** `memphis` — civic seriousness vs postmodern jokes; both read as misplaced.

## Don't copy

Johnston, New Johnston and Johnston100 outlines; the roundel; the Tube map; any TfL signage artwork. Don't build an identity that could be mistaken for TfL.
