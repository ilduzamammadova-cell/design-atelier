# Susan Kare

**Essence:** Pictograms built pixel by pixel on a coarse grid, each carrying one clear idea and, where it is allowed, a small smile.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Coarse-grid drawing | Kare mocked up the Macintosh icons as 32×32 squares in a cheap notebook of the finest graph paper she could find; each pixel was a deliberate decision, not a sample of a larger drawing. | Draw icons on a deliberately coarse grid (e.g. 24 px icon = 12×12 cells of 2 px). Every stroke snaps to cells; no anti-aliasing at 1×; review at actual size before ever zooming. |
| G2 · One idea per icon | Trash can = delete, paint bucket = fill, lasso = select. Each icon is a single, everyday metaphor with no compound decoration. | Each icon expresses one noun or verb. No stacked badges (document + gear + arrow). If the meaning needs two ideas, use a word label instead of a busier icon. |
| G3 · Personality in the margins | The smiling computer at startup and the dogcow in print dialogs put charm where the user waits or fails — never in core controls. | Reserve a small cast of "character" icons for empty states, errors and loading. Core navigation stays neutral; humour appears only at moments of pause. |
| G4 · Screen-native type | Her bitmap fonts (Chicago for menus, Geneva, New York, the monospaced Monaco) were drawn for the 72 dpi matrix rather than shrunk from print. | Pick or draw UI type that is hinted to the pixel grid at small sizes; menus in one heavier sans at one fixed size; mono for code sharing the same x-height. |
| G5 · Borrow from the public symbol commons | The Command key glyph came from an existing Scandinavian sign for a place of interest, found in a symbol reference. | For any abstract function, search signage and standards (ISO 7001, transit, folk symbols) before inventing; adapt an existing pictogram rather than coining a new metaphor. |
| G6 · Mosaic thinking | She compared bitmap graphics to mosaics and needlepoint (Stanford "Making the Macintosh" interview): form emerges from discrete cells and simple dithers. | Illustration system made of square tiles (4 px cells). The only "gradient" allowed is a dither pattern (50% checker, 25% dot). |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** 1-bit black and white on the 1984 Mac; 16-colour dithered VGA work for Windows 3.0; brighter palettes in later social-app icons.
- **Type:** Chicago, Geneva, New York, Monaco (bitmap, Apple, 1983–84); Cairo (a picture font). Free pixel alternatives: **Silkscreen**, **Pixelify Sans** (Google Fonts).
- **Form:** stair-stepped silhouettes, generous counters, 1-pixel outlines, strong figure/ground; faces made from three or four pixels.
- **Composition:** icon centred in its cell with a consistent optical margin; families share stroke weight and perspective.

## Attitude

Kare has pointed to road signs as the place to start: images understood in an instant, no manual required, and she likened bitmap work to mosaic and needlepoint. She worked from meaning first, and accepted the grid as the material rather than a limit. Her career — Apple (from 1982), NeXT (creative director, 1986), Microsoft's Windows 3.0 (including the Solitaire deck), IBM's OS/2, Facebook's virtual gifts (64×64), Pinterest (product design lead, 2015) — shows the same habits surviving every resolution change. AIGA Medal, 2018; MoMA acquired her Macintosh sketchbooks in 2015.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `otl-aicher`, `isotype`, `paul-rand` — pictogram clarity plus wit.
- **Productive tension with:** `art-nouveau` — whiplash curves forced into a pixel grid become a new stepped ornament, organic intent with digital stairs.
- **Clashes with:** `web-2-gloss` — gloss, reflections and gradients smother the one-idea, one-bit clarity that makes the icons speak.

## Don't copy

- The original Macintosh icon set (Happy Mac, trash can, paint bucket, lasso, grabber hand, the Command glyph as she drew it), Clarus the dogcow, the Chicago/Geneva/New York/Monaco bitmap drawings, the Windows 3.0 Solitaire card deck, her OS/2 icons, and her Facebook gift icons. Take the grid discipline and metaphor economy, never the drawings.
