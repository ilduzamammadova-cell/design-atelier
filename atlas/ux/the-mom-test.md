# The Mom Test

**Essence:** People will be kind to you and wrong about themselves, so learn from what they have already done, specific past episodes, time and money already spent, commitments given now, never from opinions about your idea or promises about their future.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Their life, not your idea | Once you describe your idea, people start protecting your feelings. Talk about their world and reveal the idea as late as possible: the problem is theirs to describe, the solution yours to design | Research: the first two-thirds of an interview shows no concept, mockup or feature name, only their current workflow, tools and workarounds. Taste: no atlas names, style labels or sample blends until their own work and objects have been discussed; a label offered first ("so you like Swiss?") collects agreement, not evidence |
| G2 · Last time, not usually | Generic claims (always, never), future promises (I would) and hypotheticals (I might) are fluff; in generics people describe "who they want to be". Anchor each one to a specific past instance | Research: every "do you ever / would you" answer gets "when did that last happen? walk me through it", ideally with the real file or screen open. Taste: not "what style do you like?" but "show me the last thing you saved to a moodboard, what stopped you?"; "I'm a minimalist" is followed by "open your last three projects" |
| G3 · Compliments are not data | Praise costs nothing, so it carries no information; a lukewarm "meh" is more reliable than a "wow". Deflect compliments and return to facts; if you slipped into pitching, apologise and stop | Debriefs may not record "it went well" or "they loved it", replace with a fact or delete. In-product feedback asks about the last real use ("what were you trying to do just now?"), not a rating of the idea. Taste: when a designer calls a blend "nice", ask what they would change first or which real project it would go on |
| G4 · Dig under requests and feelings | Feature requests and strong emotions are signals to explore, not instructions: find the motive, the cost and today's workaround. Someone who never looked for a fix will not adopt yours | Research: log each request with why they want it, what it would let them do and how they cope now; build for the motive (a weekly emailed summary may replace the requested dashboard). Taste: every wince or spark gets "what exactly, the colour, the spacing, the tone, a memory?", recorded verbatim, because rejections define a style |
| G5 · Three questions that scare you | Prepare the three biggest unknowns before each conversation, including one whose answer could sink the plan; start broad and zoom only after a strong signal; keep it a casual chat rather than a formal meeting | Research: a prep card per segment (a specific who-and-where) with 3 big questions, at least one about money, the decision-maker or switching, updated after every debrief. Taste: before the interview, list the three things you are least sure of in the provisional profile (is the warmth real or aspired to?) and aim questions there |
| G6 · Commitment is the only yes | A conversation either ends in a commitment or a clear next step, or it failed. The currencies are time, reputation and money; the more someone gives up, the more you can trust their words | Concept tests end with a concrete ask (pilot on their real data, intro to the budget holder, pre-order) and are logged as advanced or rejected, never "positive". Beta success = a costly action (imports real data, invites a colleague), not a satisfaction score. Taste: a direction is confirmed when the designer uses it on a real brief or shows it to a client |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** none intrinsic. Early products built this way often look like the workaround they replace, spreadsheet greys, the industry's own conventions, because they are built close to observed behaviour.
- **Type:** labels, headings and empty states in the customers' own words, lifted from interview notes; the number they already track (hours lost, money owed) set largest.
- **Form:** scrappy first versions, concierge or hand-made outputs (a manually built report, an emailed summary) before polished software; nothing built for a feature nobody committed to.
- **Composition:** screens follow the observed sequence of the real task, starting where it starts in their day. Research repositories tag notes by pain, goal, obstacle, workaround, emotion and money so they can be re-sorted when beliefs change.

## Attitude

Rob Fitzpatrick wrote the book (self-published, 2013) after learning from his own startups that friendly conversations produce expensive false positives. It makes practical the "talk to customers" part of Steve Blank's Customer Development and the Lean Startup, borrowing from design thinking, UX research and sales. The belief: finding the truth is your job, not theirs; bad news early is cheap; and deciding what to build stays with the designer, users report problems, not solutions.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `jobs-to-be-done`, `steve-krug`, `don-norman`, JTBD interviews reconstruct a real past switch; Krug watches people instead of asking; Norman holds that designers are not typical users and self-explanations are rationalisations.
- **Productive tension with:** `ideo-design-thinking`, empathy sessions and optimistic prototype showings invite compliments; run them with Mom Test discipline (facts before concepts, prototypes judged by commitments) to keep generative energy with an honest signal. The same fix applies to `kano-model` surveys: classify features from observed complaints and workarounds, not "how would you feel if…".
- **Clashes with:** `dark-patterns`, pre-ticked consent and confirm-shaming manufacture fake commitments, corrupting the only signal this method trusts.

## Don't copy

The book's sample dialogues, its "rule of thumb" lines and cheatsheet as text, its note-taking symbol set as a ready-made asset, and the cover. Take the rules and write your own scripts in your own domain and language.
