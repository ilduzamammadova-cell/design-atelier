# Jef Raskin

**Essence:** A humane interface never makes you track hidden states, never loses your work and never wastes your time — it is modeless, habit-safe and forgiving by construction.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · No modes, only quasimodes | A mode makes the same gesture mean different things; Raskin allowed only states held by continuous action, like holding Shift | No toggled "edit mode" or "selection mode": edit inline everywhere. Temporary tools are spring-loaded (hold Space to pan, release to return); if a toggle is unavoidable, its state is shown at the cursor |
| G2 · Undo, never warn | Habitual users click through confirmations without reading, so warnings protect nothing | Act immediately and offer undo: a 10-second "Deleted · Undo" toast plus a persistent history. Confirmation dialogs only for actions that truly cannot be reversed (and then type-to-confirm) |
| G3 · Your work is sacred | His first rule: the computer must not harm the user's work or let it come to harm through inaction | Continuous autosave, no Save command, drafts survive crashes and closed tabs; failed submissions keep every typed character |
| G4 · Find by content (LEAP) | On the Canon Cat (1987) you held a Leap key and typed; the cursor jumped to the match — no files, menus or launcher | A global type-to-jump that lands the cursor *in* the content, not on a results page; navigation by searching for what you remember rather than browsing folders |
| G5 · One way, made habitual | "Monotony": one obvious method per command lets habits form and frees attention | One canonical path per command; toolbar, menu and shortcut all trigger the identical behaviour; controls never move between releases |
| G6 · Guard the locus of attention | People have one locus of attention; anything that pulls it away costs work | No pop-ups over the text being edited; status appears at the periphery; errors are shown at the point of input, never in a modal |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** almost none. The Canon Cat was a beige box with a monochrome text screen; a modern echo is warm paper #F5F1E8 with charcoal #2A2A2A text and no accent.
- **Type:** monospaced document text → IBM Plex Mono or Courier Prime; or a calm serif for writing apps.
- **Form:** no icons, no toolbars, no windows on the Cat; one continuous text.
- **Composition:** a single long document as the whole environment; later ideas proposed a zooming, spatial canvas instead of files and folders.

## Attitude

Raskin, who started Apple's Macintosh project in 1979, believed interfaces could be analysed like engineering — keystroke counts, information-theoretic efficiency, cognitive load — and that a "humane" design is responsive to human needs and considerate of human frailties. He argued that "intuitive" really means "familiar", so the goal is habits that are safe to form. His son Aza carried the undo-over-warning argument into the web era.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `fitts-and-hicks-laws`, `calm-technology`, `dieter-rams` — measured efficiency, low attention cost and reduction.
- **Productive tension with:** `alan-cooper` — Cooper designs for a specific persona, Raskin for universal cognition; together they give persona-tuned surfaces on a modeless core.
- **Clashes with:** `persuasive-technology` — notifications and nudges attack the locus of attention Raskin protects.

## Don't copy

The Canon Cat's industrial design and keyboard layout, the original Macintosh project's names and marks, and the Archy interface as a visual template. Take modelessness and undo, not the beige.
