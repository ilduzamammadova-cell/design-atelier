# Aboriginal Australian art

**Essence:** Art as custodianship of Country and story, where who may paint what is governed by law, and meaning is layered for different audiences.

> **Use mode: closed to motif borrowing.** Do not imitate dot painting, rarrk (cross-hatching), x-ray figures, hand stencils, Wandjina or any Aboriginal design, palette-plus-dots "look" or symbol set. Only the abstract structural principles below may be transferred, and the right way to bring Aboriginal visual language into a product is to commission and license Aboriginal artists.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Overhead viewpoint | Many paintings show Country from above: places, journeys and relationships rather than a horizon view. | Present journeys, flows and relationships from a top-down map viewpoint with nodes and paths, drawn with your own neutral symbol set. |
| G2 · Layered disclosure | Paintings can hold public meanings on the surface and restricted knowledge for initiated viewers; some early public works deliberately obscured sacred designs. | Progressive disclosure governed by rights: a public layer for everyone, deeper layers revealed only to people with permission; permission state is visible in the UI. |
| G3 · Custodianship metadata | Rights to paint particular stories belong to particular people and groups; authorship is relational and permissioned. | Every asset carries custodian, origin and permitted-use fields that the UI shows (on hover, in exports, in credits); content without provenance cannot be published. |

## Visual facts

Described for understanding only, not as a palette to adopt: ochres (red, yellow, white, black) are used in many regions; acrylic painting on boards and canvas began at Papunya in 1971 with encouragement from teacher Geoffrey Bardon; Arnhem Land bark painting uses fine cross-hatching; Kimberley traditions include Wandjina figures, which are sacred to Worrorra, Ngarinyin and Wunambal peoples.

## Attitude

Art is a way of holding and passing on law, kinship and knowledge of Country; it is not decoration and not a generic "Indigenous style". Each painting belongs to a specific person, family and place. What designers can learn is the ethic: provenance, permission and layered audiences are part of the design.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `agnes-martin`, structurally, patient accumulation and grid-like stillness (never stylistically).
- **Productive tension with:** `swiss-international`, rational information design that adds custodianship and permission layers.
- **Clashes with:** any use of the surface look; "dot-style" graphics imitate and cause harm.

## Don't copy

Everything: works by artists such as Emily Kame Kngwarreye, Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri and the Papunya Tula painters; Yolŋu bark paintings; Wandjina; hand stencils; the Aboriginal flag (designed by Harold Thomas).

## Respect

Treat this tradition as closed to borrowing. In *Milpurrurru v Indofurn* (1994), the Federal Court awarded A$188,640, including for cultural harm, after artists' designs were copied onto imported carpets; in *Bulun Bulun v R & T Textiles* (1998) a painting of a sacred waterhole printed on fabric was treated by the community as desecration. The Productivity Commission (2022) found about two in three Indigenous-style souvenirs sold in Australia were inauthentic. Work through Aboriginal-owned art centres, follow the Indigenous Art Code and ICIP protocols (Terri Janke; Creative Australia), pay and credit the artist, language group and community. Caricature is boomerangs, didgeridoos, "dot" filters and faux-Indigenous fonts.
