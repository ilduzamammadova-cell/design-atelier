# Māori kōwhaiwhai

**Essence:** Curvilinear red, black and white patterns running along the rafters of the meeting house, where figure and ground are equal partners and every design belongs to a people and a place.

> **Use mode: structural principles only.** Kōwhaiwhai designs, the koru-based units they are built from, and all related Māori art forms are taonga (treasures) of iwi and hapū. The genes below are abstract principles; do not reproduce or imitate kōwhaiwhai motifs. Any project that wants Māori visual language must be led by Māori artists.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Equal figure and ground | Kōwhaiwhai alternate colours so that background shapes are as resolved as foreground ones; neither is leftover. | Test every pattern or illustration by inverting its colours: both versions must read as intended. Background shapes get designed, not merely left over. |
| G2 · Rhythm along a long axis | Patterns travel the length of narrow rafters (heke), reading as a continuous pulse down the house. | Long narrow rhythm strips (e.g. 24px-high bands spanning the page) as structural lines, using your own motif and repeat. |
| G3 · Three-value palette | Traditional colours are red ochre (kōkōwai), black and white. | A three-value system (one warm colour, black, white) with no tints; hierarchy made by proportion of each value. |
| G4 · Meaning tied to place | Designs are specific to iwi and locale; a pattern records a narrative of that people and house. | Pattern earns meaning from context: each section's motif refers to its own content's origin, documented in the system; no decoration without a stated reason. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** red ochre #9E2B25, black #151515, white #F4F1EA (historically soot for black, kōkōwai mixed with oil for red, natural timber as the light tone).
- **Type:** none intrinsic.
- **Form:** curvilinear, rhythmic, interlocking positive and negative shapes.
- **Composition:** long strips on rafters, often symmetrical across the ridge of the house.

## Attitude

The wharenui is understood as the body of an ancestor; rafters are ribs, and their painting carries genealogy and narrative. Contemporary Māori artists, among them Ngataiharuru Taepa, extend kōwhaiwhai into new media from within the culture. The principle worth learning is that pattern can hold kinship and history, not just rhythm.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `op-art` — figure–ground equality and optical rhythm (structurally, not stylistically).
- **Productive tension with:** `swiss-international` — rational grid that admits one long, pulsing structural band.
- **Clashes with:** `memphis` — random ornament contradicts the principle that pattern must carry meaning.

## Don't copy

Any kōwhaiwhai pattern, koru unit, tā moko (tattoo) design, whakairo (carving) or tiki; Air New Zealand's koru mark; Gordon Walters's koru paintings; works by contemporary Māori artists.

## Respect

This tradition is largely closed to outside motif use. Kōwhaiwhai belong to iwi and their houses; tā moko is personal and genealogical; carved ancestors are never decoration. The Waitangi Tribunal's Wai 262 report (*Ko Aotearoa Tēnei*, 2011) addresses Māori kaitiaki interests in taonga works. Pākehā painter Gordon Walters's stylised koru drew sustained criticism from the mid-1980s as appropriation. If a project needs Māori design, commission Māori artists and seek iwi guidance; the Toi Iho mark (launched 2002, Māori-run since 2009) signals authentic Māori-made work. Caricature is "tribal" tattoo flash, haka as mascot and generic koru swirls.
