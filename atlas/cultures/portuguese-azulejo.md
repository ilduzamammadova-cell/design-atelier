# Portuguese azulejo

**Essence:** Painted tile as everyday architecture: a modular grid that can carry a repeating carpet, a wall-length story or a whole street's identity.

## Genes

| Gene | The idea | In UI / product terms |
|---|---|---|
| G1 · Module-count scaling | 17th-century "carpet" (tapete) tiling repeats units of 2×2, 4×4 or 6×6 tiles; a pattern grows by adding modules, not by enlarging the drawing. | Patterns defined as one tile plus composite modules (1×1, 2×2, 4×4); larger containers use bigger module counts while the tile stays the same size. |
| G2 · Monochrome narrative panels | From the late 17th century, cobalt-on-white panels told stories along walls, framed by ornate borders and cartouches. | Long-scroll storytelling in single-hue illustration panels, each framed by a consistent border; text set inside cartouche-like boxes. |
| G3 · The patterned façade | 19th-century façades were clad in pattern tiles: weatherproof, reflective, and a building's identity at street scale. | Each major section of a long page gets its own subtle façade pattern, so users know which "street" they are on. |
| G4 · The grid stays visible | Images painted across many tiles accept the joint lines; the grid is part of the picture. | A faint 1px "tile seam" grid (e.g. every 120px) visible across hero illustrations and image mosaics; the grid is a material, not hidden scaffolding. |
| G5 · Abstract modernist tiles | Maria Keil's tiles for the Lisbon Metro (stations from 1959) replaced figuration with abstract geometric rhythms, partly using cheap stock tiles. | Wayfinding by pattern: each station, section or product line gets a distinct geometric tile pattern, not only a colour, which also helps colour-blind users. |

## Visual facts

- **Color:** cobalt #1E4C9A, white #F5F3EE, manganese purple-brown #5B3A4A, yellow ochre #D9A441, green #3C7A4E.
- **Type:** tiled street signs and shop fronts; for UI a classic serif such as Libre Caslon or Cormorant Garamond with a sturdy sans.
- **Form:** brush-painted majolica on tin glaze, baroque acanthus borders, geometric repeats, relief tiles on façades.
- **Composition:** interior dados roughly to shoulder height; full façades; panels framed with borders and corner pieces.

## Attitude

Tiles were affordable, washable and suited to the climate, so they moved from churches and palaces to stations, markets and ordinary houses. Portugal absorbed Islamic, Spanish, Italian, Flemish, Dutch and Chinese influences and made tile a national surface. The lesson: one modest module, endlessly recombined, can make a city legible.

## Blends

- **Amplifies:** `delftware`, `iznik-tiles`, tin glaze, blue-and-white, the tile as unit.
- **Productive tension with:** `bauhaus`, rational grid and abstraction meet hand-painted narrative.
- **Clashes with:** `psychedelic`, melting forms ignore the tile grid that gives azulejo its order.

## Don't copy

Jorge Colaço's panels at São Bento station, Porto; Maria Keil's Lisbon Metro panels; the *Great View of Lisbon* panel at the Museu Nacional do Azulejo; specific church and palace programmes.

## Respect

The tradition is open to learn from and widely produced today; credit it as Portuguese with Hispano-Moresque roots. Historic tiles are frequently stolen from buildings: never use salvaged azulejos of unknown provenance, and support the SOS Azulejo project (launched 2007 by the Polícia Judiciária museum). Caricature is the tourist kit of blue tiles plus Barcelos roosters and sardines.

## Study

- Museu Nacional do Azulejo, Lisbon (in the former Madre de Deus convent)
- SOS Azulejo project (European Heritage Award winner)
